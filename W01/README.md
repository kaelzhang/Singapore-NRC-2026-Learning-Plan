# W01 - Mission Meals And The Whole Food System

## Big Question
What does "Mission Meals" really mean, and why is a robotics competition asking us to think about food?

## Why This Week Matters
Mission Meals is not just about moving toy food pieces on a playfield. It is about a real human problem: every meal reaches a person through a food system. A food system includes farms, water, soil, sunlight, workers, machines, roads, warehouses, shops, homes, and waste handling.

For NRC, this means your robot is not just "doing a mission." Your robot is acting out one part of a real system. It might help produce food, move food, protect food, sort food, or use a better tool.

This week is a framework week. It gives you the map that will connect all other weeks.

## The One-Minute Idea
A meal is the end of a long chain. Someone had to grow ingredients, harvest them, keep them safe, move them, sell or prepare them, and avoid wasting them.

When one part of that chain breaks, people may not get enough safe and healthy food. Robots can help in some parts, but only if we understand the whole system first.

## Past, Present, Future
### Past
For most of human history, people found food by hunting, gathering, fishing, or farming near where they lived. Food was very local, and bad weather could quickly become a hunger problem.

As farming improved, people learned to store grain, irrigate fields, use animals for work, and trade food over longer distances. Food systems became bigger and more organized.

### Present
Today, food systems are global. Singapore imports more than 90% of its food from many countries and regions. A meal in Singapore may depend on farmers, ports, cold rooms, ships, trucks, supermarkets, and digital tracking.

The world also still faces hunger. SOFI 2025 estimates that about 673 million people experienced hunger in 2024. At the same time, UNEP estimates that 1.05 billion tonnes of food waste were generated in 2022. That means the problem is not only "grow more." It is also "move better," "store better," "share better," and "waste less."

### Future
Future food systems may use more indoor farms, robots, sensors, data platforms, alternative proteins, and climate-resilient crops. But technology will not automatically solve everything. We must ask: Is it affordable? Does it save energy? Who gets the benefit? What happens if the system fails?

## Deep Explanation
### 1. Food Is A System, Not A Single Object
Think about a bowl of noodles. The noodles may come from wheat. The vegetables need seeds, water, nutrients, light, and labour. The eggs may come from a farm that needs feed, temperature control, and disease prevention. The meal also needs packaging, transport, cooking, and safety checks.

So a meal is not one thing. It is the result of many connected actions.

For an eight-year-old: a food system is like a giant team that helps food travel from "growing" to "eating."

### 2. Food Security Has Four Parts
Food security means people can reliably get enough safe and nutritious food for a healthy life. It has four main parts:

- Availability: Is there enough food?
- Access: Can people afford it and reach it?
- Utilization: Is the food safe and nutritious, and can the body use it well?
- Stability: Can people keep getting food even when there are shocks?

A country can have food in supermarkets but still have access problems if some people cannot afford healthy food. A farm can produce a lot but still have stability problems if heat waves or transport failures interrupt supply.

### 3. Mission Meals Maps To Four Robot Ideas
The reference research maps the theme into four useful challenge areas:

- Food Production Infrastructure: systems that help food grow, such as farms, greenhouses, lighting, water, and sensors.
- Labour-Intensive Farming: repeated tasks that are hard, slow, tiring, or risky for people.
- Food Logistics: storage, routing, cold chain, delivery, and tracking.
- End-Effector Design: the robot's tool, such as a gripper, hook, pusher, scoop, or lifter.

These are not only competition categories. They are ways to understand the real world.

### 4. Robots Need A Clear Job
A robot cannot "fix food security" by itself. A robot needs a smaller job:

- sense a color marker,
- move to a location,
- pick up an object,
- push a model crate,
- sort objects,
- show a state with lights,
- repeat the same task reliably.

The skill is to translate a big problem into a small robot action without forgetting the big reason.

## Core Terms
### Food System
All the people, living things, tools, places, energy, data, and rules that move food from production to eating and waste handling.

Child-friendly version: the food system is the whole journey of food.

### Food Security
The condition where people can reliably get enough safe and nutritious food.

Child-friendly version: people have the food they need, not just once, but again and again.

### Availability
The amount of food that exists in a place.

### Access
Whether people can actually get the food. Money, distance, transport, and fairness matter here.

### Utilization
Whether the food is safe, nutritious, and useful for the body.

### Stability
Whether the system keeps working during shocks such as heat waves, disease outbreaks, wars, floods, or shipping delays.

### Mission Abstraction
Changing a real-world problem into a smaller task a robot can do.

Example: "food must stay cold" becomes "robot carries a cold box to the correct station."
