# W03 - Agriculture From Domestication To The Green Revolution

## Big Question
How did humans change food production from gathering wild food to managing farms, machines, and scientific crops?

## Why This Week Matters
To understand future farming, you need to know the long story of farming. Agriculture is not just "planting seeds." It is a history of humans learning to control water, soil, tools, labour, time, and information.

Mission Meals asks you to design robots for food systems. Robots are only the newest tool in a very old story.

## The One-Minute Idea
Agriculture began when people started managing plants and animals instead of only finding wild food. Over thousands of years, people invented irrigation, ploughs, storage, crop rotation, machines, fertilizers, improved seeds, and data tools. Each change helped solve one problem but often created new tradeoffs.

## Past, Present, Future
### Past
About 11,700 years ago, after the last Ice Age, some human communities began domesticating plants and animals. Domestication means changing a wild species over many generations so it becomes more useful for humans.

Later, irrigation moved water to crops. Ploughs and animal power helped farm larger areas. Storage let people keep grain for later. These changes helped towns and cities grow.

### Present
Modern agriculture uses tractors, fertilizers, pesticides, improved seeds, irrigation, satellites, sensors, and markets. The Green Revolution in the 20th century helped raise yields in many places through high-yield crop varieties, fertilizer, irrigation, and pest control.

But modern agriculture also faces problems: soil degradation, water pollution, greenhouse gas emissions, biodiversity loss, and unequal access to technology.

### Future
Future agriculture may use robotics, precision input use, climate-resilient crops, biological pest control, regenerative practices, and circular nutrient systems. The goal is not only higher yield. The goal is enough food with less damage and more resilience.

## Deep Explanation
### 1. Domestication Changed Both Plants And People
Wild plants are not designed for easy farming. Their seeds may fall off before harvest. Their fruits may be small. They may ripen at different times.

When humans saved seeds from plants they liked, generation after generation, the plants changed. Wheat, rice, maize, beans, and many fruits became more useful for human food systems.

For an eight-year-old: domestication is like choosing the best seeds again and again until the plant family slowly changes.

### 2. Irrigation Was Early Food Infrastructure
Irrigation means bringing water to crops. It can make farming more reliable, especially where rain is uncertain. But irrigation also needs canals, labour, rules, maintenance, and sometimes pumps.

This is an early example of production infrastructure: not the food itself, but the system that helps food grow.

### 3. The Green Revolution Increased Yield
The Green Revolution used crop science, fertilizer, irrigation, and pest control to increase food production, especially for staple grains. It helped many regions produce more food per hectare.

But it was not magic. It depended on inputs such as water, fertilizer, roads, credit, and knowledge. It also raised concerns about pollution, soil health, and farmer inequality where resources were not shared fairly.

### 4. Every Farming Tool Has Tradeoffs
A plough can help plant more crops, but it can disturb soil. Fertilizer can feed plants, but too much can pollute water. A robot can reduce tiring labour, but it may be expensive or hard to repair.

Good engineers ask: What problem does this tool solve, and what new problem might it create?

## Core Terms
### Agriculture
The practice of growing plants, raising animals, or managing other living food sources.

### Domestication
The long process of changing wild plants or animals so they become more useful to humans.

### Irrigation
Moving water to crops when rain is not enough or not reliable.

### Yield
The amount of crop produced from a certain area, such as kilograms per hectare.

### Staple Crop
A food that many people depend on for daily energy, such as rice, wheat, maize, or potatoes.

### Green Revolution
A period of major agricultural yield increases in the 20th century, driven by improved varieties, fertilizer, irrigation, and pest control.

### Tradeoff
When a choice helps one thing but may make another thing harder.
