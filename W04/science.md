# W04 Science - Food Security Today: Hunger, Nutrition, Affordability, And Productivity

This student document contains the science and food-system knowledge for the week. For robot building and programming work, see `robotics.md`.

## Big Question
If the world produces so much food, why do many people still face hunger or poor nutrition?

## Why This Week Matters
Mission Meals is connected to SDG 2: Zero **Hunger**. But zero hunger is not only about full stomachs. It is also about safe food, healthy diets, fair access, stable supply, and farming that can continue into the future.

This week turns the big global problem into clear pieces you can reason about. You will learn why a country can have food in shops but still have food insecurity, why calories and nutrition are not the same, why affordability matters, and why productivity alone cannot solve every food-system problem.

A robot team needs this knowledge because a robot mission should not be a random movement. It should model a real need: producing food, protecting quality, lowering waste, improving logistics, reducing hard labour, or collecting evidence.

## The One-Minute Idea
Food security has four dimensions: availability, access, utilization, and stability. A place may have enough food in total but still have hunger if people cannot afford food, if supply is interrupted, if food is unsafe, or if diets are not nutritious.

Robotics can help production and logistics, but robots must fit inside this bigger human system. A robot can move a food crate, but a secure food system also needs safe food, fair access, good nutrition, reliable supply, and people who can use the food.

Important idea: having food in the world is not enough. The right food must reach the right people safely, at a price they can pay, again and again.

## Past, Present, Future

### Past
In the past, famine was often caused by local crop failure, war, poor storage, disease, drought, floods, or weak transport. If food could not move from a place with surplus to a place with shortage, people suffered. Many societies stored grain because storage could protect people from a bad harvest.

Before modern transport and refrigeration, fresh food was harder to move far. People depended more on local seasons and preservation methods. If a harvest failed, there might not be an easy way to import enough food quickly.

But history also shows that hunger was never only a nature problem. Human decisions mattered: conflict, unfair rules, poverty, land access, trade barriers, and poor response systems could make food crises worse.

### Present
[SOFI 2025](https://www.fao.org/newsroom/detail/global-hunger-declines--but-rises-in-africa-and-western-asia--un-report/en) estimates that about 673 million people experienced hunger in 2024. It also reports that about 2.3 billion people experienced moderate or severe food insecurity. These numbers are huge, but they do not mean every place has the same problem. Some regions improved while others became worse.

Food price inflation, conflict, climate shocks, poverty, debt, disease, unequal access, and weak logistics all affect whether people can eat healthy diets. The modern world can move food across oceans, but supply chains are complex. A disruption in grain, fuel, fertilizer, shipping, animal disease control, finance, or climate can affect food prices far away.

Nutrition is also a major issue. Some people do not get enough calories. Some get enough calories but not enough protein, vitamins, minerals, or fibre. Some face unhealthy diets with too much sugar, salt, or unhealthy fat. These are different problems, but they all belong to food security and nutrition.

### Future
Future food security will depend on resilient production, smarter logistics, better nutrition, fairer access, lower waste, climate adaptation, and stronger early-warning systems. Technology will help, but policy, community action, education, affordability, and public health will matter too.

Future tools may include satellite crop monitoring, climate forecasting, heat-tolerant crops, precision irrigation, fortified foods, school meal programmes, food rescue systems, automated warehouses, farm robots, and digital traceability.

The key future challenge is balance. We need more reliable food, but not by destroying soil, wasting water, excluding small farmers, making diets unhealthy, or using energy carelessly.

## Deep Explanation

### 1. Hunger Is Not Only A Farm Problem
If there is enough rice in a country but a family cannot afford it, the family still has an access problem.

If a child eats enough calories but very few vitamins, protein, or minerals, the child may still face malnutrition.

If food is available most months but disappears during storms, conflict, or price spikes, the system has a stability problem.

If food reaches a market but is unsafe because of contamination, poor storage, or broken cold chain, the system has a utilization problem.

This is why food security uses four dimensions. One dimension alone is not enough. A strong food system must make food available, reachable, useful for health, and stable over time.

### 2. Hunger, Food Insecurity, And Malnutrition Are Different
Hunger usually means not getting enough food energy over time. It is often connected to undernourishment.

Food insecurity means uncertain or limited access to enough safe and nutritious food. A family can be food insecure even before they are visibly hungry. They may skip meals, eat less diverse food, worry about running out, or choose cheaper but less nutritious foods.

**Malnutrition** means poor nutrition. It can include undernutrition, micronutrient deficiency, overweight, and obesity. This may sound surprising, but malnutrition is not only "too little food." It means the body is not getting the right balance of nutrients.

In simple words: hunger is when the body does not get enough food energy. Malnutrition is when the body does not get the right food building blocks. Food insecurity is when getting food is uncertain or difficult.

### 3. Calories Are Energy, But Nutrition Needs More
A calorie is a unit of energy. People need energy to move, think, grow, stay warm, and keep organs working. But food is not only fuel.

Protein helps build and repair the body. Fat helps store energy and absorb some vitamins. Carbohydrates provide energy. Fibre helps digestion. Vitamins and minerals help many body processes. Iron helps carry oxygen in blood. Iodine helps thyroid function. Vitamin A supports vision and immunity. Calcium helps bones and teeth.

A meal can be high in calories but low in important nutrients. That is why food security includes nutritious food, not only enough food.

For Mission Meals, this matters because "meals" are not just objects. A meal should help a real body stay healthy.

### 4. Affordability Can Block Healthy Diets
Some foods are cheap and filling but not very diverse. Healthy diets can become too expensive when prices rise. That is why food security includes economics.

The [World Bank Food Prices for Nutrition DataHub](https://www.worldbank.org/en/programs/icp/brief/foodpricesfornutrition) tracks cost and affordability of healthy diets. This type of data helps people see whether healthy eating is possible for families after they pay for other needs.

Food prices can rise for many reasons: poor harvests, fuel costs, fertilizer costs, currency changes, transport delays, conflict, export restrictions, disease outbreaks, or extreme weather. When prices rise, families with less money are hit hardest.

Robots might reduce some costs in farming or logistics, but only if the technology is affordable, repairable, and useful at the right scale. An expensive robot that only rich farms can use may not improve food access for everyone.

### 5. Productivity Is Important But Not Enough
**Productivity** means producing more useful output from a given amount of input. A farm can be more productive per hectare, per litre of water, per worker, per dollar, or per unit of energy.

Higher productivity can help availability. If farmers grow more vegetables from the same area, local supply may improve. If logistics robots move goods accurately, warehouses may waste less time. If sensors help use water precisely, farms may produce more with less water.

But productivity alone is not the whole answer. If higher productivity damages soil, uses too much water, depends on expensive inputs, produces mostly low-nutrition food, or benefits only a few people, the system may not become truly secure.

A good food-system question is: productive for whom, by what measure, and with what side effects?

### 6. Supply Shocks Can Travel Through The System
A supply shock is a sudden event that interrupts supply. Examples include drought, flood, heat wave, war, animal disease, crop disease, port delay, export restriction, fuel price spike, or a broken cold chain.

Because modern food systems are connected, shocks can travel. A drought can reduce grain harvest. Lower grain supply can raise feed prices. Higher feed prices can affect eggs, meat, and dairy. Higher fuel prices can raise transport costs. Transport costs can raise food prices in shops.

This is why stability matters. A food system must be designed for bad days, not only normal days.

In robotics, this is similar to testing. A robot that works only when the mat is perfect, the battery is full, and the object is exactly straight is not stable. You test small shocks: slightly different object position, lower battery, small wheel slip, or timing variation.

### 7. Conflict And Climate Are Major Risk Multipliers
Conflict can stop farming, block roads, damage storage, make markets unsafe, and force people to leave home. Climate shocks can damage crops, livestock, fisheries, aquaculture, and transport infrastructure. Heat can reduce crop yields, stress animals, increase water demand, and make outdoor work dangerous.

These risks often combine. A region may face conflict and drought at the same time. A flood may damage roads just when food aid needs to move. A heat wave may reduce harvest while prices are already high.

A risk multiplier makes other problems worse. Heat, conflict, and price inflation can multiply the stress on food systems.

### 8. Early-Warning Systems Help People Act Earlier
An early-warning system watches data so people can act before a food problem becomes a crisis. Data may include rainfall, temperature, crop growth, market prices, animal disease reports, conflict reports, food stocks, satellite images, and household surveys.

An early warning is useful only if people can respond. If a system predicts drought but farmers have no support, the warning is weaker. If a system detects rising prices but there is no safety net, families may still suffer. Data must connect to action.

This is a direct robotics lesson. A sensor reading is not useful unless the program uses it. If a color sensor sees red but the robot does nothing different, the data did not become action.

### 9. Waste And Hunger Can Exist At The Same Time
It may seem strange that the world can have hunger and food waste at the same time. But this happens because food systems are about timing, location, money, safety, and behaviour.

Food can be wasted in homes, restaurants, shops, and supply chains while other people cannot afford enough food. A banana thrown away in one city does not automatically become a meal for someone far away. Rescue and redistribution need safe handling, transport, timing, volunteers or workers, storage, and trust.

Reducing waste can help food systems, but it must be done carefully. Food rescue must protect safety. Composting helps recover nutrients but should come after prevention and rescue when possible. Robots might help sort, move, or measure waste, but the best waste is often the waste that never happens.

### 10. Healthy Diets Need Culture And Choice
Food is not only nutrition. It is culture, family, religion, taste, memory, and identity. A healthy food system must respect that people do not eat nutrients as numbers. They eat meals.

For example, protein can come from eggs, fish, beans, tofu, dairy, meat, insects, algae, or novel foods, but not every community accepts every food. A future food may be scientifically safe but still need trust, good taste, fair price, and clear labelling.

This is why Mission Meals should not treat food as generic blocks only. A robot model uses blocks, but your explanation should remember that real meals are personal and cultural.

### 11. Where Robots Can Help
Robots can help food security in specific ways:

- production: seeding, watering, monitoring, harvesting, moving trays,
- labour: reducing repetitive, heavy, hot, or risky tasks,
- logistics: moving goods, docking, sorting, loading, unloading,
- safety: keeping items separated, tracking batches, reducing handling errors,
- waste: sorting, measuring, redirecting edible surplus,
- evidence: collecting repeatable data.

But robots cannot replace the need for fair access, good policy, safe food practices, healthy diets, and community trust. A robot is one tool inside a larger food system.


## Expanded Knowledge Notes

### Reading Global Hunger Numbers Carefully
A global number such as 673 million people facing hunger is important, but you should read it carefully. It is an estimate made from data, models, and surveys. It does not mean every hungry person has the same experience. Hunger can look different in different places and seasons.

Some people face chronic undernourishment over a long time. Some face acute food insecurity during a crisis. Some families eat enough in harvest season but struggle before the next harvest. Some urban families can see food in shops but cannot afford enough healthy food.

Good science uses numbers, but also asks what the numbers include, how they were measured, and what they do not show.

### Food Security Has Levels Of Severity
Food insecurity can range from worry to severe hunger. A family may first worry that food will run out. Then they may choose cheaper foods. Then adults may reduce meal size. Then children may skip meals. In severe situations, people may go whole days without eating.

This range matters because early action is better than waiting for disaster. If a system only reacts after people are starving, it is too late. Early-warning systems and social protection aim to help before the worst stage.

A robotics analogy: if your robot makes a small alignment error, fix it early. Do not wait until it crashes into every mission model.

### The Three Forms Of Malnutrition
Malnutrition is often described in three broad forms.

**Undernutrition** includes wasting, stunting, and being underweight. Wasting is low weight for height and can show recent severe food shortage or illness. Stunting is low height for age and can show long-term poor nutrition or repeated illness during growth.

**Micronutrient** deficiency means lacking important vitamins or minerals. A person may get enough calories but still lack iron, iodine, vitamin A, zinc, or other micronutrients. This is sometimes called hidden hunger because it may not look like an empty plate.

Overweight and obesity can also be forms of malnutrition when diets provide too much energy and too few healthy nutrients. Food systems can produce cheap, tasty, energy-dense foods while healthier choices remain less affordable or less convenient.

### Healthy Diets Are A Systems Problem
A healthy diet depends on more than individual choice. It depends on what foods are produced, imported, priced, advertised, stocked, cooked, served in schools, and accepted by culture.

If fresh vegetables are expensive or spoil quickly, families may buy fewer. If safe protein is costly, diets may lack protein. If ultra-processed snacks are cheap and everywhere, people may eat more of them. If families lack kitchens, refrigeration, time, or clean water, healthy cooking becomes harder.

This does not remove personal responsibility, but it shows that food environments shape choices. A good food system makes healthy choices easier.

### Affordability Means Income Plus Prices
A food is affordable only when people can pay for it after meeting other essential needs such as housing, transport, healthcare, school costs, and utilities. A healthy diet can be technically available in shops but economically out of reach.

Food price inflation can harm nutrition because families may switch to cheaper, less diverse foods. They may reduce fruits, vegetables, dairy, fish, or protein foods first because these can cost more than staple calories.

This is why food security analysis often looks at both food prices and incomes. Lower prices help, but income support and social protection may also be needed.

### Conflict Disrupts More Than Farms
Conflict can reduce food security in many ways. Farmers may not safely reach fields. Workers may be displaced. Roads, ports, bridges, warehouses, and markets may be damaged. Fuel and fertilizer may become scarce. Humanitarian groups may struggle to deliver aid. Families may lose income and homes.

Conflict can also damage trust. Food systems depend on people believing that roads are safe, contracts will be honoured, and markets will function. When trust breaks, logistics becomes harder.

For Mission Meals, conflict is not something you need to model directly in a young-student robot game. But you should understand why stability and logistics matter so much in real food systems.

### Climate Shocks Affect All Four Dimensions
Climate shocks affect availability when crops fail, animals suffer, or fish habitats change. They affect access when prices rise or incomes fall. They affect utilization when food safety risks increase because of heat, flooding, or poor water quality. They affect stability when shocks happen more often or become harder to predict.

Heat waves can reduce photosynthesis, stress livestock, increase water demand, and make outdoor labour dangerous. Floods can destroy crops and contaminate water. Drought can reduce harvests and pasture. Storms can damage ports, roads, and cold chain infrastructure.

This is why climate resilience is a food-security topic, not only an environment topic.

### Food Aid, Cash, And School Meals
When people face food insecurity, responses can take different forms. Food aid provides food directly. Cash or vouchers help families buy food in local markets. School meals support child nutrition and learning. Nutrition programmes may support pregnant women, babies, or young children.

Each method has tradeoffs. Food aid is useful when markets are broken or food is unavailable. Cash can support choice and local markets when food is available but people lack money. School meals can reach children regularly but require safe procurement, cooking, and delivery.

[WFP's supply chain page](https://www.wfp.org/supply-chain) shows that humanitarian food systems depend on planning, procurement, transport, storage, and delivery. Even emergency food is a logistics system.

### Fortification And Biofortification
Fortification means adding nutrients to food during processing. Examples can include iodized salt or flour with added vitamins and minerals. Biofortification means breeding crops to contain more nutrients, such as crops with more vitamin A, iron, or zinc.

These tools can help reduce micronutrient deficiencies, but they do not replace diverse diets. They are one part of nutrition strategy.

A robot model could represent nutrient fortification by sorting or delivering a "nutrient" resource to a food station, but the explanation should say this is a model of improving food quality, not just quantity.

### Data Dashboards And Early Warning
Modern food-security monitoring may use satellites, market price data, rainfall records, crop models, nutrition surveys, conflict reports, and household interviews. Data dashboards help experts see where risk is rising.

But data has limits. Some places have weak reporting. Satellite images may not show household income. Market prices may change quickly. A dashboard may show a warning, but people still need roads, funds, trust, and response systems.

For students, the lesson is: data is powerful, but data must connect to human action.

### Fairness And Ethics
Food security is also about fairness. If a technology improves food production but makes small farmers lose livelihoods, the system may create new social problems. If food is available only to wealthy people, access remains unequal. If a data system ignores some communities, its decisions may be unfair.

Ethical questions include:

- Who benefits from the technology?
- Who pays for it?
- Who might lose work?
- Who controls the data?
- Is the food safe?
- Is the system understandable to the people using it?

Mission Meals is a student competition, but it points to real ethical thinking. Engineering is not only making things move. It is making responsible choices.

### From Global Data To Robot Mission
To turn this week's science into a robot idea, choose one small piece of the system.

Availability mission: the robot delivers water or nutrients to a production zone.

Access mission: the robot moves food from a central storage area to a community zone.

Utilization mission: the robot sorts safe and unsafe items using a marker.

Stability mission: the robot moves backup supplies to a reserve area after a simulated shock.

Waste mission: the robot redirects edible surplus to a rescue zone and inedible waste to compost.

Each mission should include an evidence question: how many times can the robot do it correctly?


## Additional Guided Reading: Turning Big Data Into Concrete Understanding

### A Number Is Not A Person, But Every Person Counts
When you read that hundreds of millions of people face hunger, the number can feel too large to imagine. Do not let the size make you stop caring. A global number is made of individual people: children, parents, farmers, workers, and families.

At the same time, do not use one person's story to explain the whole world. Good thinking uses both: human stories for empathy and data for scale.

### A Meal Can Fail In Four Ways
Use the four food-security dimensions as a simple test.

Availability failure: the food is not there.

Access failure: the food is there, but people cannot get it or afford it.

Utilization failure: the food is unsafe or not nutritious enough.

Stability failure: the food is available today but not reliably over time.

This test helps you avoid shallow explanations. If someone says "just grow more," ask which failure they are trying to solve.

### Nutrition Is A Design Requirement
In engineering, a design requirement says what a solution must do. For food systems, nutrition is a design requirement. A meal should provide energy, protein, useful fats, vitamins, minerals, fibre, and safety.

A food system that provides many cheap calories but few nutrients has not fully succeeded. A food system that provides nutritious food that most people cannot afford has also not fully succeeded.

### Waste Reduction Is Connected To Access, But Not Automatically
Reducing waste can free resources, but it does not automatically feed hungry people. Edible surplus needs safe collection, storage, transport, matching, and quick delivery. Inedible waste needs composting or other recovery pathways.

So a waste-reduction robot mission should explain the missing steps. Sorting waste is useful, but the system must still move safe edible food to people and handle unsafe waste responsibly.

### How To Make A Strong Mission Meals Claim
Weak claim: Our robot solves hunger.

Stronger claim: Our robot models one logistics action that can support food security. It delivers a food item to the correct zone reliably, which represents how accurate distribution helps access and stability.

Strongest claim: Our robot models one logistics action that can support food security. In 10 test runs, it delivered the item correctly 8 times. The remaining failures happened when the robot started too far left, so our next improvement is a better alignment guide.

The strongest claim connects the food problem, the robot action, evidence, and improvement.

## Reading Recap
Food security is bigger than food production. A country or family can face food problems even when food exists somewhere else. The food must be available, affordable, safe, nutritious, and stable over time. Hunger, malnutrition, and food insecurity are related, but they are not the same problem. Hunger is about not enough food energy. Malnutrition is about the wrong balance of nutrients. Food insecurity is about uncertainty and difficulty in getting food.

When you design a robot mission about food security, decide which part you are modelling. A production mission is different from a delivery mission. A nutrition mission is different from a waste mission. A strong explanation names the exact weakness in the system and shows how the robot action represents one response to that weakness.

Final check: choose one food-security problem and name whether it is mainly about availability, access, utilization, stability, or a mix of several dimensions. Then explain why production alone would not fully solve it.

## Core Terms

### **Hunger**
A condition where people do not get enough food energy over time.

### **Food Insecurity**
Uncertain or limited access to enough safe and nutritious food.

### **Malnutrition**
Poor nutrition. It can mean too little food, too little of important nutrients, or too much unhealthy food.

### **Undernutrition**
A form of malnutrition where the body does not get enough energy, protein, or nutrients.

### **Micronutrient**
A vitamin or mineral needed in small amounts, such as iron, iodine, vitamin A, or zinc.

### **Healthy Diet**
A pattern of eating that gives the body enough energy and nutrients without causing harm.

### **Affordability**
Whether people can pay for what they need after considering income and other essential costs.

### **Productivity**
How much useful output a system produces from its inputs.

### **Supply Shock**
A sudden event that interrupts supply, such as drought, war, disease, export restrictions, or transport failure.

### **Early-Warning System**
A system that watches data so people can act before a food problem becomes a crisis.

### **Food Price Inflation**
An increase in food prices over time. It can make healthy diets harder to afford.

### **Social Protection**
Programmes that help people meet basic needs, such as school meals, food assistance, cash support, or nutrition support.

### **Food Utilization**
The way the body uses food, which depends on nutrition, safety, clean water, cooking, and health.
