# W06 Science Brief - Controlled Environment Agriculture And Vertical Farms

This brief keeps the main ideas from `science.md` for shorter review.

## Big Idea
**Controlled Environment Agriculture**, or **CEA**, means growing plants in a managed space such as a greenhouse, indoor farm, or plant factory. People control some or many conditions: light, water, nutrients, temperature, humidity, airflow, carbon dioxide, and cleanliness.

CEA matters for Singapore because land is limited. It can help grow selected fresh foods in cities and stacked spaces. But CEA is not magic. More control also means more responsibility, energy use, equipment, monitoring, cleaning, and repair.

## Past, Present, And Future
In the past, people protected plants with walls, shade, irrigation, terraces, glasshouses, and greenhouses. The goal was to reduce weather risk and grow valuable plants more reliably.

Today, CEA can include greenhouses, rooftop farms, hydroponic rooms, vertical farms, plant factories, and research growth chambers. Modern systems may use LED lights, pumps, sensors, nutrient tanks, fans, filters, cameras, software, and robots.

In the future, CEA may use better LEDs, smarter energy systems, recycled water, plant-growth AI, disease detection, lower-cost automation, and crops bred for indoor growth. The biggest question is often energy: can indoor farming produce useful food without using too much electricity?

## CEA Moves Weather Indoors
Outdoor farms depend on sun, rain, wind, soil, seasons, pests, and local climate. CEA tries to manage some of these conditions. A greenhouse can reduce heavy rain and wind. An indoor farm can set the light schedule. A hydroponic system can deliver nutrients directly to roots.

But when humans move weather indoors, humans must take care of it. If a pump fails, roots may dry. If a fan fails, humidity may rise and disease may spread. If lights fail, photosynthesis slows. If a sensor is wrong, the system may make bad choices.

Control is useful only when the farm can keep control reliably.

## Different Levels Of Control
Not all CEA systems are the same.

A shade house may only reduce strong sunlight and pests. A greenhouse may protect from rain and wind while adding irrigation, ventilation, or cooling. An indoor vertical farm may control almost all light, water, nutrients, temperature, humidity, airflow, and carbon dioxide. A research growth chamber may control conditions very precisely for experiments.

Higher control can improve consistency, but it usually costs more and is more complex. A good farm chooses the level of control that fits the crop and place.

## Vertical Farming Uses Space Upward
**Vertical farming** grows plants in stacked layers. This can produce more plants per floor area, which is useful where land is scarce.

Stacking also creates problems. Lower layers may need artificial lights. Lights create heat. Air must move between shelves. Water and nutrients must reach each layer. Workers or robots must move trays safely. Disease can spread if cleaning is poor.

Vertical farming works best for compact, fast-growing, higher-value crops such as leafy greens, herbs, and seedlings. It is usually not a good choice for large low-cost grains such as rice or wheat.

## Root-Zone Systems
**Hydroponics** grows plants without soil using water with dissolved nutrients. The roots may sit in flowing water, deep water, a thin nutrient film, or a support material.

**Aeroponics** keeps roots hanging in air and sprays them with nutrient mist. It gives roots oxygen, but if the mist stops, roots can dry quickly.

**Aquaponics** links fish and plants. Fish waste can become plant nutrients after helpful microbes change it. This system must keep fish, plants, and microbes healthy at the same time.

All root-zone systems need water, nutrients, oxygen, suitable pH, correct temperature, and cleanliness.

## Climate Control Is A System
**Climate control** includes temperature, humidity, airflow, light, and sometimes carbon dioxide. These parts affect each other.

High heat can stress plants. Very dry air can pull too much water from leaves. Very humid air can slow transpiration and increase disease risk. Poor airflow can create hot or wet spots on shelves. Carbon dioxide can support photosynthesis, but it must be managed safely.

This is why CEA is a whole system, not one machine.

## Water Recycling And Biosecurity
CEA can save water by recirculating nutrient solution. **Recirculation** means water is captured and used again. This can reduce waste, but problems can also recirculate. Disease, salts, wrong pH, or clogged filters can spread through the loop.

**Biosecurity** means stopping pests, diseases, or contamination from entering and spreading. Indoor farms still need clean tools, safe water, pest checks, worker hygiene, and good seedling sources.

## Mission Meals Connections
A CEA robot can move plant trays, deliver water or nutrient tokens, sort stressed plants, activate a light station, move produce to packing, separate clean and dirty tools, or carry a tray without tipping.

## Extra Details To Remember
A vertical farm can have many tiny local climates, called microclimates. One shelf may be warmer because it is close to lights. Another shelf may be more humid because airflow is weak. Another area may get less carbon dioxide because plants use it and air does not mix well. Fans and layout matter because still air hides problems.

Growers sometimes think about how strongly air pulls water from leaves. You do not need the formula, but the idea is useful: dry air pulls water quickly; very wet air may slow water movement and increase disease risk. Temperature and humidity should be managed together.

Crop stages need different care. Seedlings need gentle conditions. Growing leafy greens need enough light and nutrients. Flowering or fruiting crops may need support, pollination, and different timing. A CEA farm may have germination, nursery, growth, harvest, packing, and storage zones.

Energy is often the hardest tradeoff. Indoor farms may need electricity for lights, pumps, fans, cooling, dehumidifying, sensors, lifts, and robots. Better LEDs, smart schedules, efficient pumps, heat management, and good crop choice can help.

Cleaning is production. Algae, biofilm, pests, blocked pipes, and dirty tools can reduce growth and food safety. A robot moving dirty tools away from clean zones can represent a serious farm task.

## Review Example: A Shelf Farm
A one-shelf plant factory may need a light, tray, water tank, pump, fan, sensor, and cleaning plan. If the light works but the pump fails, plants still suffer. If the pump works but airflow is poor, disease risk may rise. CEA succeeds when the parts work together.

## Key Takeaways
CEA can help cities grow selected foods by managing plant conditions. Vertical farms save land but may use energy. Hydroponics can use water efficiently but needs careful monitoring. Control creates responsibility.
