# W06 Science - Controlled Environment Agriculture, Hydroponics, And Vertical Farms

This student document contains the science and food-system knowledge for the week. For robot building and programming work, see `robotics.md`.

## Big Question
Can people grow food indoors, in cities, and even in stacked layers?

## Why This Week Matters
Singapore has limited land. **Controlled Environment Agriculture**, or CEA, is one way to grow selected crops in smaller spaces by controlling light, water, temperature, humidity, airflow, and nutrients.

This week connects plant science to urban farming technology. W05 explained what plants need. W06 asks how humans can build systems that provide those needs in a controlled way.

CEA is important for Mission Meals because it turns food production into an engineered environment. A robot may move trays, check sensors, deliver resources, sort plants, or model a farm station. To make that meaningful, you need to understand what the farm is controlling.

## The One-Minute Idea
Controlled Environment Agriculture means growing plants in a managed space such as a greenhouse, indoor farm, or plant factory. Vertical farming stacks growing layers upward. **Hydroponics** grows plants without soil. These systems can save land and sometimes water, but they may need electricity, skilled operators, careful monitoring, cleaning, and repair.

Simple idea: CEA is like making a special room for plants where people manage the weather, water, food, and light.

## Past, Present, Future

### Past
People have protected plants for a long time using walls, glasshouses, shade, irrigation, terraces, and season extension. The goal was to reduce risk from bad weather, protect valuable plants, and grow food or flowers outside the normal season.

Greenhouses became important because they let sunlight in while protecting plants from wind, cold, and pests. Early protected farming still depended heavily on the sun and outdoor climate.

### Present
Modern CEA uses sensors, climate control, LED lighting, nutrient dosing, pumps, cameras, software, fans, filters, and sometimes robots. In Singapore, CEA and urban farms are important because they can produce selected fresh foods close to consumers despite limited land.

CEA is not one technology. A simple greenhouse, a rooftop farm, a hydroponic room, a vertical farm, a plant factory, and a research growth chamber are all different. They control different variables at different levels.

### Future
Future CEA may use smarter energy systems, better LED recipes, lower-cost automation, recycled water, plant-growth AI, disease detection, improved varieties for indoor growth, and building-integrated farms. Space agriculture research may also improve Earth farming because it asks how to grow food in very controlled environments.

The biggest question may be energy: can indoor farms be productive without using too much electricity? Other questions include cost, crop choice, labour skills, maintenance, food safety, and consumer acceptance.

## Deep Explanation

### 1. CEA Tries To Move Weather Indoors
Outdoor farms depend on sun, rain, wind, soil, seasons, pests, and local climate. CEA tries to control some of these variables. Instead of waiting for the right weather, a farm can set light timing, temperature, humidity, airflow, water flow, nutrient concentration, and sometimes carbon dioxide.

More control can reduce some risks. A greenhouse can protect plants from heavy rain. An indoor farm can grow at night using lights. A hydroponic system can deliver nutrients directly to roots. A screenhouse can reduce pest pressure.

But moving weather indoors also moves responsibility indoors. If a pump fails, plants may dry out. If a fan fails, humidity may build up. If lights fail, photosynthesis drops. If a sensor is wrong, the system may make bad decisions.

### 2. CEA Has Different Levels Of Control
Not all controlled farms are equally controlled.

A shade house may only reduce sunlight and protect from some pests.

A greenhouse may control rain, wind, temperature, humidity, irrigation, and sometimes ventilation.

An indoor vertical farm may control nearly all light, water, nutrients, temperature, humidity, airflow, and CO2.

A research growth chamber may control conditions very precisely for experiments.

Higher control can improve consistency, but it usually increases cost and complexity. A farm should choose the level of control needed for the crop and business, not the most complex system possible.

### 3. Vertical Farming Uses Space Upward
If land is scarce, one idea is to stack plant trays in layers. This increases production per floor area. A vertical farm can produce many layers of leafy greens in a building.

Stacking creates engineering problems. Lower layers may not receive sunlight, so artificial lights are often needed. Lights create heat, so cooling and airflow matter. Water and nutrients must reach each layer. Workers or robots must move trays safely. Disease or algae can spread if cleaning is poor.

Vertical farming is strongest for certain crops, especially fast-growing, high-value, compact crops such as leafy greens, herbs, and seedlings. It is usually not a good fit for large grain crops like rice or wheat because those crops need much more area and lower production cost.

### 4. Hydroponics, Aeroponics, And Aquaponics Are Root-Zone Designs
Hydroponics gives roots water with dissolved nutrients. The roots may sit in flowing water, a nutrient film, deep water, or a support medium such as rockwool, coco coir, or clay pellets.

**Aeroponics** hangs roots in air and mists them with nutrient solution. This can give roots oxygen, but it needs reliable misting. If the mist stops, roots can dry quickly.

**Aquaponics** links fish and plants. Fish waste contains nutrients that can support plant growth after microbes transform the waste into plant-usable forms. The system must keep fish, plants, and microbes healthy at the same time.

These systems all need balance. The root zone needs water, nutrients, oxygen, suitable pH, suitable temperature, and cleanliness.

### 5. Climate Control Means More Than Temperature
Climate control includes temperature, humidity, airflow, light, and sometimes carbon dioxide. These variables interact.

Temperature affects plant metabolism. Too cold can slow growth. Too hot can stress plants.

Humidity affects transpiration. Very dry air can make plants lose water quickly. Very humid air can reduce transpiration and encourage disease.

Airflow helps mix air, reduce hot spots, move humidity, and strengthen plants. Poor airflow can create disease-friendly zones.

Carbon dioxide is a raw material for photosynthesis. Some greenhouses enrich CO2, but this must be managed carefully and safely.

This is why CEA is a system, not a single machine.

### 6. Lighting Is A Major Design Choice
Outdoor farms use sunlight for free, although weather and seasons vary. Indoor farms often use LEDs. LEDs can provide specific wavelengths and timing, but they require electricity and produce heat.

Lighting design includes spectrum, intensity, photoperiod, distance from plants, uniformity, and energy efficiency. A light recipe is a planned combination of light colour, amount, and timing for a crop.

[NASA plant hardware references](https://science.nasa.gov/biological-physical/focus-areas/plant-biology/hardware/) show how research systems can use red, blue, green, white, and far-red LEDs. Space plant systems care about light because every watt of energy matters.

For Earth farms, energy cost can decide whether an indoor farm succeeds. A beautiful plant factory that uses too much electricity may not be sustainable or affordable.

### 7. Water Recycling Is Powerful But Needs Safety
CEA can sometimes use water efficiently because water can be recirculated. Hydroponic systems may capture and reuse nutrient solution. This can reduce waste compared with some outdoor systems.

But recycling water means problems can also recycle. If pathogens enter the system, they may spread. If salts build up, roots may be stressed. If pH drifts, nutrients may become less available. If filters clog, flow may change.

Good water management includes monitoring, filtration, cleaning, nutrient adjustment, and sometimes disinfection. This is why sensor data and maintenance matter.

### 8. Biosecurity And Cleanliness Matter
**Biosecurity** means preventing harmful organisms from entering or spreading in a production system. In CEA, pests and diseases can still appear. Workers, tools, water, seeds, air, and plant material can carry problems.

Indoor systems can be protected, but once a pest or disease enters, dense plant spacing can let it spread. Clean benches, footbaths, tool cleaning, pest screens, careful seedling sources, and monitoring can help.

In Mission Meals, a sorting or inspection mission can represent biosecurity. A robot might separate healthy and unhealthy plants, or move a "contaminated" item away from safe food.

### 9. Automation Helps Repetition And Monitoring
Indoor farms repeat many tasks: moving trays, checking sensors, dosing nutrients, cleaning systems, harvesting, and packing. Robots and automation can help if the task is clear and frequent.

Automation may include conveyor belts, tray elevators, dosing pumps, climate controllers, camera systems, robotic arms, mobile robots, or software alerts. Simple automation can be very valuable. Not every farm needs a humanoid robot.

A good automation question is: Which repeated task creates the most time, safety, quality, or reliability problem? That is the task to study first.

### 10. The Tradeoff Is Control Versus Cost
More control can reduce some risks, but it can also increase cost and complexity. A vertical farm may save land and reduce pesticide use, but it may need electricity for lights and cooling. A hydroponic system may save water, but it needs pumps and monitoring. A greenhouse may protect crops, but it can overheat without ventilation.

The best system depends on crop value, local climate, land cost, energy cost, labour, skills, market demand, and food-security goals.

For Singapore, CEA can help selected local production, especially fresh vegetables and other suitable crops. It is not a complete replacement for all imports. It is one tool in a wider resilience system.

### 11. CEA And Space Farming Teach Each Other
Space farming is an extreme version of CEA. Astronauts cannot depend on outdoor soil, rain, bees, or normal sunlight in the same way as Earth farms. Systems such as NASA's [VEGGIE](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/veggie/) and other plant research hardware study how to provide light, water, nutrients, oxygen, and root support in space.

Space systems must be compact, reliable, energy-aware, and safe. These are also useful goals for Earth indoor farms. Lessons from space research can support Earth farming, while Earth agriculture provides knowledge for future space missions.

A CEA mission can model:

## Expanded Knowledge Notes

### Why CEA Is Not The Same As Ordinary Indoor Decoration
A plant in a classroom pot is indoors, but it is not automatically controlled environment agriculture. CEA means the growing environment is managed for production. The goal is not only keeping a plant alive. The goal is producing food consistently, safely, and efficiently.

That means CEA needs measurement and control. It asks: How much light? How many hours? What nutrient concentration? What pH? What temperature? What humidity? What airflow? What cleaning routine? What harvest schedule?

### Greenhouse Versus Plant Factory
A greenhouse uses sunlight but protects plants with a structure. It may add irrigation, fans, screens, heaters, cooling pads, shade, or sensors. It still interacts strongly with outdoor weather.

A plant factory with artificial lighting is much more separated from outdoor weather. It may use stacked shelves and LED lights. It can be placed in a warehouse-like space, but it depends more on electricity and climate-control systems.

Neither is automatically better. A greenhouse may be energy-efficient in a suitable climate. A plant factory may be useful where land is scarce or outdoor conditions are difficult. The right choice depends on crop and place.

### Humidity And Vapour Pressure Deficit
Humidity is the amount of water vapour in air. Plants lose water through transpiration. If air is very dry, plants may lose water too quickly. If air is too humid, transpiration may slow and disease risk may increase.

Advanced growers may use a concept called vapour pressure deficit, or VPD. It describes how strongly air pulls water from leaves. You do not need to calculate it now, but you should understand the idea: plants respond to the drying power of air, not temperature alone.

This is why CEA controls temperature and humidity together.

### Airflow Prevents Hidden Problems
Airflow moves heat, humidity, and carbon dioxide. Without airflow, one shelf may be hot, another humid, and another low in carbon dioxide. Still air can make disease more likely.

Fans are simple but important. They help mix air and reduce microclimates. A microclimate is a small local climate inside a larger space.

A vertical farm has many possible microclimates because shelves, lights, trays, and plants can block air movement.

### Crop Choice Decides The System
CEA is often used for leafy greens, herbs, seedlings, and some high-value crops. These crops can grow quickly, fit in stacked systems, and be sold fresh.

Large staple crops such as rice, wheat, and maize are harder to grow economically in indoor vertical farms because they require much more space, light, and low cost per kilogram. This does not mean indoor grain is impossible in research. It means it is usually not the practical food-security choice for a city.

Good food-system thinking asks which crop fits which production method.

### Water Loops Need Monitoring
A recirculating hydroponic farm may reuse water. This can save water, but the loop must be monitored. Plants remove nutrients at different rates. Evaporation changes concentration. pH can drift. Microbes can grow. Filters can clog.

A water loop is like a shared classroom supply box. If everyone uses different supplies and nobody checks the box, it becomes unbalanced.

Sensors and regular testing help farmers adjust the solution before plants suffer.

### Energy Is Often The Hardest Tradeoff
Indoor farms may need energy for lights, pumps, fans, cooling, dehumidification, sensors, computers, lifts, and robots. The energy source matters. If electricity is expensive or high-carbon, the farm's cost and environmental impact rise.

Energy efficiency can improve through better LEDs, better building insulation, smarter schedules, heat recovery, efficient pumps, and crop choices that need less light.

When someone says vertical farms save land, ask the next question: what energy do they use, and how is that energy produced?

### Automation Can Be Simple Or Complex
Automation does not have to mean a fully autonomous robot. A timer that turns lights on and off is automation. A pump controlled by a water-level sensor is automation. A dosing system that adjusts nutrients is automation. A conveyor that moves trays is automation.

Complex robots can help, but simple reliable automation may be more useful than a complicated machine that breaks often.

For NRC, this is important. A simple attachment that works reliably may be better than a complex mechanism that succeeds only once.

### Food Safety In CEA
CEA food still needs food safety. Water must be clean. Tools must be cleaned. Workers need hygiene. Harvesting and packing must avoid contamination. If leafy greens are eaten raw, safety is especially important.

Indoor systems can reduce some outdoor contamination risks, but they can also create dense conditions where a problem spreads quickly. Control does not remove responsibility.

A robot mission that separates clean and dirty tools, sorts safe and unsafe produce, or moves harvested food to a safe zone can model this idea.

### Singapore And CEA
Singapore's land constraints make CEA attractive for selected foods. Urban farms can be closer to consumers, which may reduce transport time for fresh produce. They can also create local production capacity that helps resilience.

But Singapore must also consider energy cost, business sustainability, consumer demand, skilled workers, and competition with imports. CEA is one part of Singapore Food Story, not the whole story.

### CEA Mission Examples
Tray movement mission: The robot moves a plant tray from germination to growth zone. Science explanation: plants have growth stages and controlled environments can be adjusted.

Nutrient dosing mission: The robot delivers a nutrient marker to a hydroponic tank. Science explanation: roots need dissolved nutrients in correct concentration and pH.

Climate warning mission: The robot detects a colour marker and moves a warning block. Science explanation: sensors can trigger action when temperature or humidity is outside range.

Harvest mission: The robot moves mature produce to packing. Science explanation: timing and gentle handling protect quality.

Biosecurity mission: The robot moves contaminated tools away from plant zones. Science explanation: disease prevention is part of controlled production.


## Additional Guided Reading: CEA Design Choices

### Open Loop Versus Closed Loop
An open-loop system acts without checking the result. A timer turns a pump on for five minutes every day whether plants need water or not.

A closed-loop system measures and responds. A sensor checks moisture or water level, then the controller decides whether to pump.

CEA often uses closed loops because conditions change. But closed loops need trustworthy sensors and good thresholds.

### Centralized Versus Local Control
A centralized system controls many shelves or rooms from one controller. This can be efficient, but one failure can affect many plants.

Local control means each zone or shelf has some independent control. This can be more resilient but may cost more.

Food systems and robots both use this tradeoff. One central program can be simple. Smaller local checks can make a system safer.

### Crop Stages Need Different Conditions
A seedling, growing plant, flowering plant, and harvest-ready plant may need different conditions. Seedlings may need gentle light and steady moisture. Leafy greens may need strong enough light for growth. Fruiting crops may need pollination and support.

This means a CEA farm may have zones: germination, nursery, growth, hardening, harvest, packing, and storage.

A robot tray-moving mission can model moving crops between stages.

### Cleaning Is Production
Cleaning may sound boring, but in food production it is critical. Algae, biofilm, pests, disease, and blocked pipes can reduce crop health and food safety.

A farm that ignores cleaning may produce less food or unsafe food. Cleaning schedules, tool separation, water treatment, and worker hygiene are part of production infrastructure.

A Mission Meals robot could model moving dirty tools away or delivering clean tools to the farm zone.

### Why CEA Still Needs Farmers
A controlled system still needs people. Farmers choose crop varieties, set recipes, inspect plants, clean equipment, repair pumps, compare data, harvest, pack, and sell food.

Automation changes the farmer's job, but it does not remove knowledge. A good CEA farmer understands both plants and machines.


## Final CEA Reminder: Control Creates Responsibility

CEA sounds powerful because it gives humans more control. But control also creates responsibility. If a farm controls light, it must manage energy. If it controls water, it must monitor pumps and quality. If it controls humidity, it must prevent disease. If it stacks plants, it must provide airflow and access.

This is a good Mission Meals lesson. A robot that controls a mission object also becomes responsible for accuracy and safety. Control is useful only when the system can keep control reliably.

A strong CEA explanation should name both benefit and tradeoff. Example: "Vertical farming can use less land for selected crops, but artificial lighting and climate control can require significant energy." That sentence is more mature than simply saying vertical farming is good.

## Reading Recap
Controlled-environment agriculture changes where and how some crops can grow. Instead of accepting outdoor weather, people manage light, water, nutrients, airflow, temperature, humidity, and space. Hydroponics and vertical farms can be useful where land is limited, but they still require energy, equipment, maintenance, skilled decisions, and careful safety practices.

The key idea is control with tradeoffs. More control can improve consistency, but it can also increase complexity. A sensor can measure a condition, but the system must respond correctly. A pump can deliver nutrient solution, but it can also fail. When you build a robot model, show both sides: what the controlled system improves and what it must monitor to remain reliable.

## Core Terms

### **Controlled Environment Agriculture**
Growing plants in a managed environment where conditions such as light, temperature, humidity, airflow, water, and nutrients are controlled.

### **Greenhouse**
A structure that protects plants while allowing sunlight in and climate control.

### **Plant Factory**
An indoor growing facility that uses high control, often with artificial lighting and stacked layers.

### **Vertical Farming**
Growing crops in stacked layers to use vertical space.

### **Hydroponics**
Growing plants without soil by giving roots water with dissolved nutrients.

### **Aeroponics**
Growing plants with roots hanging in air and misted with nutrient solution.

### **Aquaponics**
A system that links fish farming and plant growing, using fish waste nutrients for plants after microbial processing.

### **Root Zone**
The area around plant roots where water, oxygen, nutrients, and microbes interact.

### **Light Recipe**
A planned combination of light spectrum, intensity, and timing for plant growth.

### **Climate Control**
Managing temperature, humidity, airflow, and sometimes carbon dioxide.

### **Biosecurity**
Actions that reduce the risk of pests, diseases, or contamination entering and spreading.

### **Recirculation**
Reusing water or nutrient solution inside a system.

### **Energy Efficiency**
How much useful output is gained from each unit of energy.

### **Tradeoff**
A choice where improving one goal may make another goal harder.
