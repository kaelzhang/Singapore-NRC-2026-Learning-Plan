# W11 Science - Food Loss, Food Waste, Circular Economy, And Recovery

This student document contains the science and food-system knowledge for the week. For robot building and programming work, see `robotics.md`.

## Big Question
Why do people waste food while others are hungry, and what can systems do about it?

## Why This Week Matters
Producing more food is not enough if huge amounts are lost or wasted. Mission Meals should include the idea that food has value at every step. Robots can help sort, rescue, move, measure, or process food before it becomes waste.

Food waste is also a systems problem. It connects farming, logistics, safety, shops, homes, labels, habits, storage, packaging, and recovery systems. A banana wasted at home carries the water, land, labour, transport, and cooling used before it arrived.

## The One-Minute Idea
Food loss usually happens before food reaches consumers, such as during harvest, storage, processing, or transport. Food waste usually happens at retail, restaurants, schools, or homes. A circular economy tries to keep materials useful for as long as possible.

Do not just throw value away. Try first not to waste it. If safe food is extra, share it. If it cannot feed people, recover value safely.

## Past, Present, Future

### Past
People traditionally preserved food by drying, fermenting, salting, pickling, smoking, and storing grain. These methods reduced waste before refrigeration and global transport.

Food scraps were often fed to animals, composted, or returned to soil. In many communities, wasting edible food was avoided because food required hard labour to produce.

### Present
[UNEP's Food Waste Index Report 2024](https://www.unep.org/resources/publication/food-waste-index-report-2024) estimates 1.05 billion tonnes of food waste in 2022 at retail, food service, and household levels. UNEP and FAO also discuss food lost after harvest and before retail. Waste creates climate and resource problems because land, water, energy, labour, packaging, and transport were already used to produce the food.

Food waste can happen in wealthy and less wealthy countries, but the reasons may differ. In some places, storage and transport losses are major. In others, household and food-service waste are major.

### Future
Future waste reduction may use smarter date labels, food rescue networks, better cold chain, AI demand forecasting, improved storage, composting, insect bioconversion, anaerobic digestion, packaging redesign, and consumer education. The best solution is prevention: do not create avoidable waste in the first place.

Future food systems may also design waste out from the beginning. That means better portion planning, better packaging size, better harvest timing, better inventory data, and circular recovery of unavoidable organic material.

## Deep Explanation

### 1. Food Loss And Food Waste Are Different
Food loss happens earlier in the supply chain. Examples:

- crops left unharvested,
- grain spoiled in storage,
- fish damaged during transport,
- fruit rejected during processing,
- food damaged before retail.

Food waste happens closer to consumers. Examples:

- uneaten school lunch,
- expired food at home,
- restaurant leftovers,
- supermarket food thrown away,
- over-prepared buffet food.

The exact definitions can vary, but the important idea is that food exits the human food system at different stages for different reasons.

### 2. Waste Is Also Wasted Resources
When food is wasted, the system also wastes:

- water used to grow it,
- land used to produce it,
- fertilizer and energy,
- labour,
- packaging,
- transport,
- cold storage,
- money,
- time.

Food waste is therefore not only a bin problem. It is a hidden resource problem. The waste bin is the last place where the problem becomes visible.

### 3. Food Waste Has Climate Effects
Food waste can contribute to greenhouse gas emissions in several ways. Producing food uses energy, land, fertilizer, and transport. If wasted food goes to landfill, it can produce methane as it breaks down without oxygen. Methane is a powerful greenhouse gas.

Reducing food loss and waste can therefore help climate goals, food security, and resource efficiency at the same time.

This connects W11 to W12: climate risk affects food systems, and food waste also affects climate pressure.

### 4. Circular Economy Keeps Materials In Use
A circular economy tries to reduce the "take, make, throw away" pattern. In food systems, this can mean:

- prevent waste,
- redistribute edible surplus,
- upcycle ingredients,
- feed safe scraps to animals where allowed,
- compost plant waste,
- use anaerobic digestion,
- recover nutrients safely,
- redesign packaging and portions.

Circular does not mean everything is automatically safe to reuse. Food safety and contamination matter.

### 5. The Wasted Food Scale Puts Prevention First
The [EPA Wasted Food Scale](https://www.epa.gov/sustainable-management-food/food-recovery-hierarchy) ranks actions from more preferred to less preferred. The most preferred actions keep food for its intended purpose: feeding people.

Prevention is usually best because it avoids wasting resources in the first place. Donation or redistribution can help when safe edible food is surplus. Upcycling can create products from by-products. Composting and anaerobic digestion can recover value from material that cannot feed people. Landfilling and sending food down drains are least preferred.

For students: composting is useful, but preventing edible food waste is usually better.

### 6. Date Labels Can Confuse People
Some food is thrown away because people misunderstand date labels. A date label may refer to quality, safety, or stock rotation depending on the system and country. If people treat every date as a danger signal, edible food may be wasted. If people ignore safety dates, illness risk can increase.

Clear labels and education can reduce waste. Food safety still matters. Smell, appearance, storage history, and label meaning all matter.

Mission idea: sort food by date marker and send items to eat-soon, donate, compost, or unsafe disposal zones.

### 7. Cosmetic Standards Can Create Waste
Sometimes food is rejected because it is the wrong size, shape, or appearance, even if it is safe and nutritious. Curved carrots, small apples, or spotted but safe produce may be rejected by markets or consumers.

This is a systems problem because appearance standards affect farmer income, food availability, and waste. Education and alternative markets can help.

Robot idea: sort produce by safety and use, not only beauty. A slightly imperfect but safe item can go to processing, donation, or cooking.

### 8. Food Rescue Needs Safety And Logistics
Food rescue means redirecting safe edible surplus to people instead of waste. It sounds simple, but it needs timing, refrigeration, transport, volunteers or workers, packaging, records, and trust.

Not all surplus food is safe to rescue. Cooked food, chilled food, allergens, and opened packages may need special care. Food rescue must protect people from illness.

A robot mission can model food rescue by moving safe surplus to a community zone while sending unsafe or contaminated items elsewhere.

### 9. Upcycling Turns By-Products Into Useful Products
Upcycling means turning a lower-value material or by-product into a useful product. For example, fruit pulp, grain by-products, or vegetable trimmings may become ingredients, animal feed where safe and allowed, or other products.

Upcycling needs food safety, quality control, and a real market. It is not enough to make something possible; people must want and trust the product.

### 10. Composting Returns Nutrients To Soil
Composting is a controlled process where organic material breaks down with oxygen into compost. Compost can improve soil organic matter and nutrient cycling.

Composting needs the right mix of materials, moisture, air, and time. If poorly managed, compost can smell bad or attract pests. Composting is useful for inedible organic material, but edible food should usually be prevented or rescued first.

### 11. Anaerobic Digestion Recovers Energy
Anaerobic digestion breaks down organic material without oxygen. It can produce biogas and digestate. Biogas can be used for energy. Digestate may be used as a soil amendment if safe and properly managed.

Anaerobic digestion can be useful for food waste that cannot feed people. But it requires infrastructure, management, and contamination control.

### 12. Sorting Matters
Before you can rescue, recycle, compost, or digest, you must sort. Is it edible? Is it safe? Is it packaging? Is it wet waste? Is it contaminated? Is it an allergen risk? Is it suitable for animal feed under local rules?

Sorting is a real robot problem. Robots can use colour, shape, weight, barcodes, cameras, or sensors. But food waste can be messy, wet, and mixed, making sorting difficult.

### 13. Measurement Makes Waste Visible
You cannot reduce what you do not measure. Food waste measurement can track how much, where, why, and what type of food is wasted.

A school can measure plate waste. A shop can measure unsold items. A warehouse can measure spoilage. A farm can measure unharvested crops. Data helps target action.

In Mission Meals, a robot can model measurement by sorting items into counted zones and recording results.

### 14. Waste Mission Examples
Prevention mission: move correct portions to avoid overproduction. Science idea: planning reduces avoidable waste.

Rescue mission: deliver safe surplus to a community zone. Science idea: edible food should feed people.

Sorting mission: separate edible, compostable, and contaminated items. Science idea: recovery depends on correct sorting.

Compost mission: move inedible plant waste to compost. Science idea: nutrients can return to soil.

Data mission: count waste markers after a simulated meal. Science idea: measurement guides improvement.


## Expanded Knowledge Notes

### Why Waste Happens At Farms
Food can be lost on farms because of weather, pests, disease, labour shortage, low market price, cosmetic standards, harvest timing, lack of storage, or lack of transport.

Sometimes a crop is safe and edible but not harvested because the price is too low to pay for labour. Sometimes a storm damages produce. Sometimes a buyer wants only a certain size or shape. Sometimes there are not enough workers during a short harvest window.

This shows that food loss can be economic, biological, logistical, or social.

### Why Waste Happens In Storage
Storage losses can happen because of moisture, heat, pests, mould, poor containers, broken cold rooms, or weak inventory records. Grain can spoil if too moist. Fruit can decay if too warm. Frozen food can lose quality if temperature fluctuates.

Good storage is a science and engineering problem. It needs containers, temperature control, humidity control, pest control, airflow, cleaning, and records.

A robot mission can model storage by moving food to correct zones and rotating older items first.

### Why Waste Happens In Shops And Restaurants
Shops may waste food because they over-order, demand changes, products expire, cold rooms fail, packaging is damaged, or customers reject imperfect items. Restaurants may waste food through over-preparation, large portions, trimming, buffet leftovers, and plate waste.

Data can help. If a shop knows which items often expire, it can adjust ordering or discounts. If a restaurant measures plate waste, it can change portion sizes or menu planning.

### Why Waste Happens At Home
Household food waste can happen because people buy too much, forget food in the refrigerator, misunderstand date labels, cook too much, store food poorly, or reject leftovers.

Home waste is difficult because it depends on habits. Small actions help: planning meals, checking the fridge before shopping, storing food correctly, using leftovers safely, understanding labels, and sharing surplus.

### Edible, Inedible, Unsafe, And Unwanted
These words are different.

Edible means people can eat it.

Inedible means people normally do not eat it, such as some peels, bones, shells, or spoiled material.

Unsafe means it may harm people because of contamination, spoilage, allergens, or other hazards.

Unwanted means people do not want it, even if it is edible and safe.

A food-rescue system must never confuse unwanted with unsafe. Unwanted but safe food may be rescued. Unsafe food should not be given to people.

### The Best Next Use
For each surplus or waste item, ask the best next use:

1. Can we prevent it from becoming surplus next time?
2. Is it safe and edible for people now?
3. Can it be upcycled into another food product safely?
4. Can it become animal feed under rules?
5. Can it become compost or energy?
6. Must it be disposed because it is contaminated?

This is circular thinking. It keeps value high for as long as possible.

### Sorting Contamination
Contamination makes recovery harder. Plastic in food scraps can ruin compost. Food mixed with chemicals may be unsafe. Allergen mixing can make donated food risky. Packaging stuck to food can make processing harder.

Robots and humans both need sorting systems. Labels, bins, colours, sensors, and training help. But systems must be simple enough for people to use correctly.

### Insect Bioconversion
Some systems use insects, such as black soldier fly larvae, to convert organic by-products into insect protein and frass. Frass is insect waste that may be used as a soil input if managed safely and legally.

This can be part of circular food systems, but it requires safety control, regulation, and market acceptance. Insects are not a magic bin. They are living organisms in a managed production system.

### Packaging Tradeoffs
Packaging can reduce food waste by protecting food, extending shelf life, and providing information. But packaging can also create material waste.

A cucumber wrapped in plastic may look wasteful, but if the wrapping greatly reduces spoilage, the total environmental result may be more complicated. A large package may use less material per gram but cause waste if a family cannot finish it.

Good design looks at the whole system, not only one object.

### Waste And Food Security
Reducing waste can support food security, but it does not automatically solve hunger. Safe surplus must be collected, stored, transported, and matched to people quickly. Food rescue needs logistics and trust.

This is why W11 connects to W10. Waste reduction is not only sorting bins. It is also movement and timing.

### Waste Mission Design Checklist
When designing a waste mission, ask:

- Is the item edible, inedible, unsafe, or unwanted?
- What is the best next use?
- What sorting information does the robot need?
- What happens if the robot puts it in the wrong zone?
- How will the team measure success?

A good waste mission teaches priority, not just cleanup.


## Additional Guided Reading: Waste Reduction In Daily Life And Robotics

### Classroom Food Waste
A classroom or team snack can produce avoidable waste. Students may take too much, open food they do not finish, throw away edible leftovers, or mix food with packaging so it cannot be composted.

A simple classroom measurement can teach systems thinking. Count unopened food, edible leftovers, inedible scraps, packaging, and contaminated waste separately. Then ask which part can be prevented next time.

### Plate Waste
Plate waste is food served to a person but not eaten. In schools, plate waste can happen because portions are too large, food is unfamiliar, lunch time is too short, students dislike the taste, or students take food they do not plan to eat.

Reducing plate waste may involve smaller portions with seconds available, taste education, better scheduling, or student feedback. It is not only a bin problem.

### Overproduction
Overproduction means making more food than needed. Restaurants, buffets, bakeries, and school canteens may overproduce because they do not want to run out. Running out disappoints customers, but overproduction creates waste.

Forecasting and flexible menus can help. Food rescue can help with safe surplus, but prevention is still better.

### Storage At Home
Home storage affects waste. Some foods need refrigeration. Some need dry storage. Some fruits release ethylene gas that can speed ripening. Some foods should be sealed to reduce drying.

Students do not need to memorize every storage rule, but they should see that waste can happen after food is bought. The consumer is part of the food system.

### Compost Is Not A Perfect Excuse
Compost is useful for inedible scraps, but it does not erase all the resources used to grow edible food. If edible rice, vegetables, or bread are composted, the water, land, labour, cooking energy, and transport were still wasted.

This is why the waste hierarchy places prevention and feeding people above composting.

### Robot Sorting Is Hard Because Waste Is Messy
Clean LEGO blocks are easy to sort. Real food waste can be wet, smelly, mixed, soft, sticky, sharp, or contaminated. Packaging may hide food. Labels may be missing. Liquids may leak.

Robots that sort waste need sensors, grippers, cleaning, and safety design. Sometimes the best solution is not a robot at the bin, but better upstream design so waste streams stay separate.

### Upstream And Downstream Thinking
Upstream means earlier in the system. Downstream means later. If waste appears in a bin, downstream sorting can help. But upstream prevention may be better.

Example: If students throw away unopened milk, downstream recycling does not solve the main problem. Upstream, the school can ask why students took milk they did not drink.

This is a powerful Mission Meals idea: do not only clean up. Find why waste happened.

### Waste Mission Evidence
A waste mission should record:

- number of items correctly sorted,
- number of edible items rescued,
- number of contaminated items kept out,
- number of runs without mixing streams,
- time needed,
- failure causes.

The goal is not just moving waste. The goal is better decision-making about value.


## Final Waste Reflection

Food waste is emotionally simple but technically complex. It feels wrong to throw away food, but preventing waste requires planning, storage, safety, logistics, culture, labels, portions, data, and habits.

A robot can help sort waste, but sorting is late in the system. Sometimes the better solution is earlier: buy less, cook the right amount, harvest at the right time, improve storage, discount near-expiry food, or donate safe surplus.

### Avoiding A Common Mistake
Do not say, "Composting solves food waste." Composting is useful, but it is not the top solution for edible food. A better statement is: "Prevention and safe rescue should come before composting when food is still edible and safe. Composting is useful for inedible or unavoidable organic material."

A final notebook sentence for this week: "The best waste solution protects the highest remaining value safely." For edible safe food, the highest value is feeding people.


## Tiny Waste Audit Practice

For one day, notice food that almost became waste. Did someone save leftovers? Did fruit get eaten before it spoiled? Did a lunch portion become too large? Did packaging help protect food or create extra trash?

Do not use this as a blame exercise. Use it as a systems exercise. Ask what made the waste likely: portion size, storage, timing, taste, label confusion, forgetfulness, or lack of a rescue path.

Then choose one prevention idea. Prevention ideas are often simple: smaller first portions, a visible leftovers shelf, clearer labels, better storage containers, a shopping list, or checking the refrigerator before buying more.

A waste mission becomes stronger when it includes prevention, not only cleanup. In your robot explanation, say whether the robot models prevention, rescue, sorting, composting, or measurement.


A final practical rule: sort by next use, not by disgust. Some food scraps are inedible but useful for compost. Some imperfect food is edible and should not be wasted. Some food looks fine but may be unsafe because of temperature abuse or contamination. A good waste system uses evidence and safety rules instead of guessing.

For Mission Meals, this means the robot should not simply push all "waste" into one corner. It should show a decision: rescue, upcycle, compost, digest, or dispose. That decision is where the science lives.

One more useful distinction is avoidable versus unavoidable waste. Avoidable waste could have been eaten or prevented with better planning. Unavoidable waste includes parts people normally do not eat, such as some shells or bones, although cultures differ. This distinction helps students avoid unrealistic goals. A zero-waste system still has biological leftovers; the question is whether those leftovers are handled safely and usefully. In your waste audit, separate cause from blame. A person may throw away food because the system made waste likely: confusing labels, oversized portions, poor storage, no sharing option, or too little time to eat. Better design changes the conditions so the better choice becomes easier. This is systems thinking, not scolding. Measurement should also separate weight, count, and reason. Ten tiny scraps and one full sandwich are different problems. A kilogram of unavoidable peels is different from a kilogram of unopened meals. Good data helps choose the right intervention instead of guessing from the bin alone. Finally, remember that waste reduction is a design challenge, not a guilt contest. The best systems make good choices easy: clear bins, clear labels, right-sized portions, visible leftovers, safe donation paths, and reliable collection. Good design prevents confusion before waste happens, especially when students check the cause carefully.

## Reading Recap
Food waste is not only a rubbish problem. It is wasted land, water, labour, energy, transport, money, and nutrition. The best action is usually prevention: do not create avoidable waste in the first place. If safe food is extra, food rescue may help. If food cannot feed people, it may still become animal feed, compost, soil support, or energy, depending on local rules and safety.

A robot waste mission should respect this hierarchy. Sorting everything into trash is weak thinking. A stronger mission separates edible surplus, unsafe food, compostable material, packaging, and landfill waste. The robot can model how better information and better sorting protect value before it is lost.

Final check: before choosing compost, ask whether the item could have been prevented, safely rescued, reused, or upcycled. The best waste solution often happens before the bin.
 Also explain which resources are being protected: food, water, energy, labour, packaging, transport, and money.
## Core Terms

### Food Loss
Food that is lost before it reaches shops or consumers.

### Food Waste
Food that is thrown away at retail, food service, or household stages.

### Circular Economy
A system that keeps materials useful and reduces waste.

### Upcycling
Turning a lower-value material or by-product into a useful product.

### Food Rescue
Redirecting safe edible surplus food to people instead of waste.

### Composting
A controlled process where organic material breaks down with oxygen into compost.

### Anaerobic Digestion
Breaking down organic material without oxygen to produce biogas and digestate.

### Waste Hierarchy
A ranking of actions: prevent first, then reuse or redistribute, recycle or recover, and dispose last.

### Contamination
When unwanted material makes food or waste streams unsafe or hard to reuse.

### Methane
A greenhouse gas that can be produced when organic material breaks down without oxygen.

### Shelf Life
How long food keeps acceptable safety and quality under certain storage conditions.

### Surplus
Extra food beyond what is needed or sold.

### By-Product
Material left from making another product that may still have value.
