# W11 Science Brief - Food Loss, Food Waste, Circular Economy, And Recovery

## Big Idea
Food waste is not only rubbish. It is also wasted water, land, energy, labour, packaging, transport, money, and nutrition. A banana thrown away at home carries the resources used to grow, move, store, and sell it.

The best waste solution usually protects the highest remaining value safely. If edible food can be prevented from becoming waste, prevent it. If safe edible food is extra, rescue it. If it cannot feed people, recover value safely when possible.

## Past, Present, And Future
In the past, people used drying, salting, pickling, smoking, fermenting, grain storage, and careful cooking to reduce waste. Scraps were often fed to animals, composted, or returned to soil.

Today, large amounts of food are wasted at homes, shops, restaurants, schools, and food service. Food is also lost earlier during harvest, storage, processing, and transport. The reasons are different in different places: weak storage, poor roads, pests, overbuying, confusing labels, large portions, cosmetic standards, or cold-chain failure.

In the future, waste reduction may use better date labels, food rescue networks, demand forecasting, cold chains, storage, composting, insect bioconversion, anaerobic digestion, packaging redesign, and consumer education. Prevention remains the best goal.

## Food Loss And Food Waste
**Food loss** usually happens before food reaches consumers. Crops may be left unharvested, grain may spoil in storage, fish may be damaged in transport, or fruit may be rejected during processing.

**Food waste** usually happens closer to consumers. Examples include uneaten school lunch, expired food at home, restaurant leftovers, supermarket waste, and over-prepared buffet food.

The exact definitions can vary, but the important idea is that food leaves the human food system at different stages for different reasons.

## Waste Also Affects Climate
Producing food uses energy, land, water, fertilizer, transport, and cooling. If food is wasted, those resources were used for nothing.

When food waste goes to landfill and breaks down without oxygen, it can produce **methane**, a powerful greenhouse gas. Reducing food waste can support food security, resource efficiency, and climate goals at the same time.

## Circular Economy And Waste Hierarchy
A **circular economy** tries to keep materials useful and reduce the "take, make, throw away" pattern. In food systems, this can mean preventing waste, rescuing safe surplus, upcycling by-products, feeding safe scraps to animals where allowed, composting, using anaerobic digestion, or recovering nutrients.

The **waste hierarchy** puts actions in order. Prevention comes first. Feeding people comes before composting. Composting and energy recovery are useful for inedible or unavoidable material, but they should not become an excuse to waste edible food.

## Edible, Inedible, Unsafe, And Unwanted
These words are not the same.

**Edible** means people can eat it. **Inedible** means people normally do not eat it, such as some peels, shells, or bones. **Unsafe** means it may harm people because of contamination, spoilage, allergens, or temperature problems. **Unwanted** means people do not want it, even if it is safe and edible.

A good waste system must not confuse unwanted with unsafe. Unwanted but safe food may be rescued. Unsafe food must not be given to people.

## Food Rescue, Upcycling, Composting, And Digestion
**Food rescue** sends safe edible surplus to people instead of waste. It needs timing, cold storage, transport, packaging, records, and trust. Not all surplus food is safe to rescue.

**Upcycling** turns a lower-value by-product into a useful product. It needs safety, quality control, and a real market.

**Composting** breaks down organic material with oxygen into compost that can support soil. It needs air, moisture, time, and the right mix of material.

**Anaerobic digestion** breaks down organic material without oxygen and can produce biogas and digestate. It can recover energy from food waste that cannot feed people, but it needs infrastructure and safety control.

## Sorting And Measurement
Before rescue, compost, digestion, or disposal, the system must sort. Is the item safe? Edible? Inedible? Packaged? Wet? Contaminated? Allergen-risk? Suitable for the next use?

Measurement makes waste visible. A school can measure plate waste. A shop can measure unsold items. A farm can measure unharvested crops. Data helps people choose the right solution instead of guessing from the bin.

## Mission Meals Connections
A robot can model prevention, rescue, sorting, composting, digestion, or measurement. It might send safe surplus to a rescue zone, contaminated food to inspection, inedible plant scraps to compost, and packaging to another stream.

The robot should sort by best next use, not by "trash" as one big category.

## Extra Details To Remember
Waste can happen on farms because of weather, pests, disease, labour shortage, low market price, cosmetic standards, harvest timing, lack of storage, or lack of transport. Sometimes food is edible but not harvested because the cost of picking it is too high.

Storage waste can happen because of moisture, heat, pests, mould, broken cold rooms, poor containers, weak airflow, or bad inventory records. A warehouse that forgets older food in a corner may create waste even if the food was once safe.

Home waste often comes from buying too much, forgetting food, misunderstanding labels, cooking too much, storing food poorly, or refusing leftovers. These are habit and design problems, not only personal blame.

Contamination makes recovery harder. Plastic mixed with food scraps can ruin compost. Food mixed with chemicals may be unsafe. Allergen mixing can make donation risky. Clear bins, labels, colours, training, and simple rules help people sort correctly.

Packaging has tradeoffs. Packaging can protect food, extend shelf life, and carry labels. It can also create material waste. Good design looks at the whole system. Too little packaging may cause more food spoilage. Too much packaging may waste materials.

Avoidable waste is food that could have been eaten or prevented with better planning. Unavoidable waste includes parts people normally do not eat, though cultures differ. A realistic waste system separates the cause from blame and chooses the best next use.

Upstream thinking asks why waste happened before it reached the bin. Downstream sorting can help, but upstream prevention is often stronger.

## Review Example: One Sandwich
A sandwich left uneaten may be safe, unsafe, or only unwanted. If it was kept clean and cold, rescue may be possible. If it sat warm too long, safety may fail. If it is mixed with dirty packaging, recovery becomes harder.

The same item can have different next uses depending on history. That is why labels, timing, storage, and sorting matter. A waste robot should not guess from appearance alone when safety information is needed.
A strong waste mission names both the material and the reason. Bread crust, unopened milk, bruised fruit, and dirty packaging are different problems. Different problems need different next actions.

## Key Takeaways
Food waste is a system problem. The best solution often happens before the bin: better planning, storage, labels, portions, forecasting, and rescue. Recovery is useful, but edible safe food should usually feed people first.

Final check: choose one wasted item. Ask whether it could have been prevented, rescued, upcycled, composted, digested, or disposed safely.
