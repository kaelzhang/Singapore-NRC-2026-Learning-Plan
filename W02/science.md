## Big Question
How does a small city-state like Singapore keep food available when most food comes from somewhere else?

## Why This Week Matters
Mission Meals is a global theme, but we are preparing for Singapore NRC. Singapore is a special food-system case because it has very little farmland and imports more than 90% of its food. That does not mean Singapore is helpless. It means food resilience must be designed carefully.

This week helps you understand why local production, import diversity, stockpiling, and partnerships all matter.

## The One-Minute Idea
Singapore cannot simply grow every type of food locally. Instead, it builds resilience using several strategies at once:

- buy food from many places,
- grow selected foods locally,
- keep important food stocks,
- build partnerships with other countries,
- use science and technology to do more with less land.

## Past, Present, Future

### Past
Singapore used to have more farms. In earlier decades, local production included eggs, poultry, pork, vegetables, and fish. But as the city grew, land became scarce and Singapore relied more on imported food.

### Present
Singapore's Food Story 2 uses four pillars: import diversification, growing local, stockpiling, and global partnerships. The 2035 local production targets focus on 20% of local fibre consumption and 30% of local protein consumption. In this context, "fibre" means foods such as leafy vegetables, fruited vegetables, beansprouts, and mushrooms. "Protein" means eggs and seafood in the revised targets.

This is a practical choice. Rice, poultry, and many meats are difficult to produce locally at scale because of land, feed, climate, and cost constraints.

### Future
Future Singapore food resilience may include more climate-controlled farms, smarter fish farms, disease monitoring, shared farm infrastructure, stockpile optimization, and better demand for local produce. Future food systems may also include new proteins, but Singapore requires safety assessment before novel foods can be sold.

## Deep Explanation

### 1. Import Dependence Is A Design Problem
If a country imports most of its food, it must ask:

- What if one supplier country has a drought?
- What if a disease affects animals?
- What if shipping routes are delayed?
- What if food prices rise quickly?
- What if people panic-buy?

Food resilience means the system can bend without breaking.

### 2. Diversification Means Not Putting All Food In One Basket
Import diversification means buying from many countries and regions. If one route or supplier has trouble, another source may still work.

For an eight-year-old: if you need markers for class, it is safer if more than one friend can bring markers.

### 3. Growing Local Is A Buffer, Not A Complete Replacement
Local farms give Singapore a fresh and reliable buffer during disruptions. But because land is limited, Singapore focuses on foods that make sense to grow efficiently, such as vegetables, eggs, and seafood.

This is why technology matters. Indoor farms, high-density growing systems, sensors, automation, and controlled environments can help farms produce more with less space.

### 4. Stockpiling Buys Time
Some foods can be stored. Rice, canned food, frozen proteins, and other essentials can help during temporary disruptions. A stockpile does not solve every problem, but it gives the system time to respond.

### 5. Partnerships Make Food Security International
Food systems cross borders. Agreements, trusted trade routes, and shared safety standards help countries keep food moving during shocks.

## Core Terms

### Food Resilience
The ability of a food system to keep working during disruptions.

### Import Diversification
Getting food from many sources instead of depending too much on one source.

### Local Production
Food grown or produced inside the country.

### Stockpiling
Keeping a reserve of important food so the system has backup during disruptions.

### Global Partnership
Cooperation with other countries or organizations to keep food supply stable and safe.

### Fibre
In Singapore's revised food targets, this refers to selected plant foods such as vegetables, beansprouts, and mushrooms.

### Protein
In Singapore's revised targets, this mainly refers to eggs and seafood.

### Novel Food
A food without a long history of being eaten safely, such as some cultured meat or insect products. It needs safety assessment before sale.
