# W05 Science Brief - Plant Growth, Photosynthesis, Light, Water, And Nutrients

## Big Idea
Plants are living food factories. They use light, carbon dioxide, water, nutrients, and their own cells to grow leaves, roots, stems, flowers, fruits, and seeds. A farm, greenhouse, or robot system that supports plants must respect plant biology.

The main question is not "Can we force plants to grow?" The better question is "Can we give plants the right conditions, notice stress early, and avoid wasting resources?"

## Past, Present, And Future
In the past, farmers learned plant needs by observation. They watched seasons, soil, rain, leaf colour, pests, and harvest results. They saved seeds and adjusted planting through experience.

Today, growers can measure light, **spectrum**, water, humidity, temperature, carbon dioxide, soil moisture, pH, electrical conductivity, nutrient levels, and plant stress. Hydroponic farms and greenhouses can control many of these conditions.

In the future, farms may use smarter lighting, recycled water, plant sensors, disease detection, AI crop models, climate-resilient plants, biological pest control, nutrient recovery, and robots that handle plants gently.

## Photosynthesis Makes Plant Food
**Photosynthesis** is the process plants use to make sugar from light, carbon dioxide, and water. Chlorophyll in leaves captures light energy. The plant uses that energy to build sugars, which become food for growth.

A simple version is:

```text
light + carbon dioxide + water -> sugar + oxygen
```

Leaves are like solar panels, food factories, and breathing surfaces at the same time. They take in carbon dioxide through tiny openings called **stomata**. Stomata also let water vapour escape, so the plant must balance getting carbon dioxide with saving water.

## Light Has Amount, Colour, And Time
Plants need enough light, but light is not only bright or dim. **Light intensity** means how strong the light is. **Spectrum** means the colours or wavelengths in the light. **Photoperiod** means how many hours of light and dark the plant receives.

Blue and red light are very important for photosynthesis, but green and far-red light can also affect plant shape and signals. Growers may use LED light recipes to control growth. A **light recipe** is a planned mix of colour, strength, and timing.

Light has a tradeoff. More light can increase growth if other needs are met, but indoor lights use electricity and create heat. Too much or poorly managed light can waste energy or stress plants.

## Water Moves And Cools The Plant
Water is a raw material for photosynthesis, but it also moves nutrients, keeps cells firm, and cools leaves through **transpiration**. Transpiration is water leaving the plant as vapour.

Water moves upward through **xylem**, which is plant tissue like tiny pipes. Sugars move through **phloem** from leaves to places that need energy, such as roots, fruits, or growing tips.

Too little water can make plants wilt and slow growth. Too much water can push oxygen out of the root zone and harm roots. Plants need balance.

## Roots Need More Than Water
Roots need water, nutrients, oxygen, space, and a suitable temperature. They also interact with helpful microbes in soil.

Important plant nutrients include nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, calcium, magnesium, sulfur, iron, zinc, and others. **Nitrogen** helps leaves and proteins. **Phosphorus** helps energy transfer and roots. **Potassium** helps water balance and many plant functions.

A **limiting factor** is the need in shortest supply. If a plant lacks nitrogen, adding more light may not fix the problem. If roots lack oxygen, adding more fertilizer may not help.

## Soil And Hydroponics
**Soil** is a living system of minerals, organic matter, air, water, roots, and microbes. Soil texture, pH, nutrients, drainage, and organic matter all affect plant growth.

**Hydroponics** grows plants without soil by giving roots water with dissolved nutrients. Hydroponic systems must manage pH, nutrient strength, water flow, oxygen, cleanliness, and disease risk. Hydroponics can save space and sometimes water, but it needs pumps, monitoring, and maintenance.

## Plant Stress Is A Signal
Plants cannot talk, but they show signs. Yellow leaves, curled leaves, slow growth, wilting, spots, weak stems, poor roots, or uneven colour may mean stress. The cause could be water, nutrients, heat, pests, disease, light, pH, or root damage.

Sensors can help, but a sensor reading is not the full answer. A good grower asks what changed and checks several clues.

## Mission Meals Connections
A robot can support plant systems by moving trays, delivering water tokens, sorting stressed plant markers, activating lights, checking a sensor station, or moving harvest-ready produce gently.

The robot explanation should name the plant need. For example: "Our robot moves a tray from germination to the growth zone because young seedlings and older plants need different conditions."

## Extra Details To Remember
Plants have source and sink parts. A **source** makes or releases sugar, such as a mature leaf doing photosynthesis. A **sink** uses or stores sugar, such as a growing root, young leaf, flower, fruit, or seed. The plant must move energy from source to sink through phloem.

Leaf colour gives clues, but it is not a complete answer. Yellow leaves may mean nitrogen shortage, old leaves, root trouble, too much water, too little light, disease, or another stress. A plant detective checks several clues before deciding.

pH matters because it changes nutrient availability. A nutrient may be present in the water or soil but hard for roots to take up if pH is wrong. In hydroponics, growers often measure pH and electrical conductivity. **Electrical conductivity**, or EC, gives a clue about dissolved salts and nutrient strength, but it does not tell exactly which nutrient is missing.

Temperature affects plant speed. Cool conditions may slow growth. Very hot conditions can increase water loss and stress. Humidity affects how strongly air pulls water from leaves. Airflow helps move heat, humidity, and carbon dioxide around the plant.

Roots need oxygen because they are living tissue. In waterlogged soil, roots may not get enough oxygen. In hydroponics, water may need aeration or movement so roots can breathe.

Helpful microbes can support plants by cycling nutrients, protecting roots, or improving soil structure. Harmful microbes can cause disease. This is why soil health and cleanliness both matter.

The limiting factor idea also helps robotics. If a robot has great code but a weak attachment, the attachment limits the mission. If the attachment is strong but navigation is poor, navigation limits the mission. Systems improve when you find the true limiting factor.

For Mission Meals, plant handling should be gentle. Real seedlings and leafy greens can bruise, tear, dry, or wilt. Even if the model is a block, the robot explanation should show respect for living materials.

## Review Example: Diagnosing A Plant
Imagine two plants look small. Plant A has dry soil and curled leaves. Plant B has wet soil, yellow leaves, and weak roots. They are both small, but they may not need the same fix. Plant A may need water. Plant B may have too much water and too little root oxygen.

This is why plant science needs observation before action. A robot mission that delivers the same resource to every plant may miss the real problem. A stronger mission sorts plant markers by need: water stress, nutrient stress, disease inspection, or harvest-ready.

Plants are living systems. They do not respond like LEGO blocks. Timing, balance, and gentle handling matter.
A simple classroom plant check can include leaf colour, soil or water condition, root space, light level, and recent changes. The answer should come from several clues, not one guess. This is the same habit used in science and engineering.

The safest answer often begins with observation: what changed, what does the plant show, and which need is most likely limiting growth now? Then check twice before acting.

## Key Takeaways
Plants need light, carbon dioxide, water, nutrients, oxygen around roots, and suitable temperature. Growth is a balance, not a checklist. The weakest need can limit the whole plant. Hydroponics and controlled farms are useful only when the system monitors and maintains plant needs.

Final check: choose one plant need and explain what happens if the plant gets too little or too much of it.
