# W12 Science - Climate Risk, Extreme Heat, Water Stress, And Resilient Agriculture

This student document contains the science and food-system knowledge for the week. For robot building and programming work, see `robotics.md`.

## Big Question
How can food systems keep working when heat, drought, floods, and weather shocks become stronger?

## Why This Week Matters
Food systems depend on climate. Crops, livestock, fish, workers, storage, and transport can all be affected by extreme weather. A Mission Meals robot should be designed with resilience in mind.

This week connects climate science to agriculture and engineering. It also connects back to everything you have studied: plant biology, controlled environments, sensors, logistics, waste, and food security.

## The One-Minute Idea
Climate risk means the chance that climate conditions will harm a system. Extreme heat can reduce crop yields, stress animals, make outdoor work dangerous, warm water bodies, increase storage problems, and disrupt logistics. Resilient agriculture tries to prepare, adapt, and recover.

In simple words, resilience means a system can get bumped and still keep going, or recover quickly after it falls.

## Past, Present, Future

### Past
Farmers have always dealt with weather risk. They used planting calendars, seed saving, irrigation, terraces, shade, crop diversity, food storage, migration, and trade to survive uncertain seasons.

Traditional knowledge often included careful observation of clouds, winds, soil moisture, plant behaviour, and animal behaviour. People did not have satellites, but they watched patterns closely.

### Present
FAO and WMO warn that extreme heat is a major and growing threat to agrifood systems. Heat affects crops, livestock, aquaculture, forests, and workers. Climate shocks can also combine with conflict, prices, pests, disease, and supply-chain problems.

Climate risk is already a food-security issue. A heat wave can lower yields. Drought can reduce water supply. Floods can damage crops and contaminate water. Storms can block roads and ports. Marine heat waves can affect fisheries and aquaculture.

### Future
Future resilience may use heat-tolerant crops, improved irrigation, shade systems, early-warning services, climate data, protected cultivation, crop diversity, cooler logistics, emergency stockpiles, insurance, and better water governance. But adaptation has limits if warming continues to increase.

Future food systems will need both adaptation and mitigation. Adaptation helps systems cope with climate impacts. Mitigation reduces greenhouse gas emissions or increases carbon storage to slow climate change.

## Deep Explanation

### 1. Climate Risk Is Hazard Plus Exposure Plus Vulnerability
A hazard is a dangerous climate event, such as heat, drought, flood, storm, or sea-level rise.

Exposure means people, crops, animals, infrastructure, or supply routes are in the place where the hazard happens.

Vulnerability means how easily they can be harmed. A crop with no irrigation may be more vulnerable to drought. A worker without shade may be more vulnerable to heat. A city depending on one import route may be more vulnerable to transport disruption.

Risk becomes high when hazard, exposure, and vulnerability combine.

### 2. Heat Affects Plant Biology
Plants have temperature ranges where they grow well. Too much heat can reduce photosynthesis, damage flowers, dry soil, increase respiration, and increase water stress. Flowering and pollination can be especially sensitive in many crops.

Heat often works together with water stress. When air is hot and dry, plants lose water faster. Stomata may close to save water, but then less carbon dioxide enters, and photosynthesis can slow.

Heat can also increase pest and disease pressures in some regions. Warmer conditions may allow some pests to spread or reproduce faster.

### 3. Heat Affects Animals And Workers
Livestock can suffer heat stress. Animals may eat less, produce less milk or eggs, grow more slowly, become less fertile, or become ill. Chickens, pigs, cattle, and fish all have temperature limits.

Agricultural workers can also face heat stress. Outdoor work in high heat can be dangerous, especially when humidity is high, shade is limited, or rest and water are insufficient.

This is why climate resilience is also about people. A food system cannot be resilient if workers are unsafe.

### 4. Water Stress Is More Than No Water
Water stress can mean:

- not enough rain,
- rain at the wrong time,
- irrigation water too expensive,
- groundwater falling,
- salty water,
- polluted water,
- floods that damage roots,
- competition between farms, cities, and ecosystems,
- water infrastructure failure.

Too little water harms crops. Too much water can also harm crops by drowning roots, spreading disease, washing away soil, or contaminating food.

### 5. Floods Can Create Food Safety Problems
Floods do not only knock plants over. Floodwater can carry sewage, chemicals, pathogens, and debris. Crops touched by contaminated floodwater may become unsafe. Floods can also damage storage, roads, bridges, cold rooms, and power systems.

This connects climate risk to food utilization and logistics. A crop may physically exist but become unsafe or impossible to move.

### 6. Climate Risk Travels Through Supply Chains
A heat wave can reduce crop yield. Lower yield can raise prices. Higher prices can reduce access to healthy food. Heat can also affect workers, transport, cold storage, and electricity demand.

A drought can reduce grain harvest. Grain shortage can raise animal feed prices. Feed prices can affect eggs, meat, fish, and dairy. A climate event in one region can affect food prices in another region through trade.

This is why climate risk is a chain, not a single event.

### 7. Early Warning Helps People Act Earlier
Weather forecasts, heat alerts, pest monitoring, water data, and crop stress maps can give people time to protect crops, adjust work hours, move stock, irrigate, or prepare supplies.

FAO's climate services work includes satellite-based agricultural stress monitoring such as ASIS, the Agricultural Stress Index System. These tools help identify areas where vegetation may be under stress.

But warning is useful only if people can act. A farmer who receives a drought warning still needs water access, money, seeds, advice, or support. Data must connect to response.

### 8. Adaptation Means Changing To Reduce Harm
Adaptation can happen at many scales.

Plant scale: heat-tolerant or drought-tolerant varieties.

Farm scale: shade nets, mulch, irrigation scheduling, soil organic matter, crop diversity, water harvesting, drainage, protected cultivation.

Worker scale: shade, rest breaks, safer work hours, water, heat alerts.

Supply-chain scale: cold storage, backup routes, emergency stocks, temperature monitoring.

City or country scale: import diversification, stockpiling, water planning, climate services, insurance, and support for farmers.

### 9. Mitigation Means Reducing Climate Pressure
Mitigation reduces greenhouse gas emissions or increases carbon storage. In food systems, mitigation can include reducing food loss and waste, improving fertilizer use, protecting forests, improving manure management, saving energy, using renewable energy, and changing some production and consumption patterns.

Mitigation and adaptation are different but connected. Adaptation helps with impacts. Mitigation reduces future risk.

### 10. Resilience Uses Diversity And Backup
A resilient system usually does not rely on only one crop, one supplier, one route, one storage method, or one technology. Diversity and backup give options when a shock happens.

Examples:

- crop diversity reduces dependence on one crop,
- import diversification reduces dependence on one supplier,
- stockpiles buy time,
- protected cultivation reduces exposure to some weather,
- sensors provide early warning,
- local production can buffer some disruptions,
- waste reduction makes existing food go further.

### 11. Controlled Environments Are Adaptation Tools With Tradeoffs
Greenhouses, shade houses, vertical farms, and hydroponics can protect crops from some weather risks. They may reduce exposure to heavy rain, pests, or heat, depending on design.

But controlled environments can also depend on electricity, cooling, pumps, and materials. During a power outage, a high-control farm may become vulnerable quickly.

This is a recurring lesson: every adaptation has tradeoffs.

### 12. Climate Resilience Mission Examples
Heat warning mission: a robot reads a heat marker and moves crops to shade. Science idea: heat stress can harm plants and workers.

Water stress mission: a robot delivers water only to drought-stress zones. Science idea: water timing and targeting matter.

Flood safety mission: a robot separates contaminated flood-zone food from safe food. Science idea: climate events can create food safety risks.

Backup route mission: a robot chooses an alternate delivery path after a blocked route. Science idea: resilient logistics needs backup.

Stockpile mission: a robot moves emergency supplies after a simulated shock. Science idea: stability depends on preparedness.


## Expanded Knowledge Notes

### Climate Is Not The Same As Weather
Weather is what happens over short time periods: today's rain, this week's heat, tomorrow's storm. Climate is the long-term pattern of weather in a place.

Farmers care about both. Weather affects daily decisions: irrigate today, harvest before rain, stop work during dangerous heat. Climate affects long-term decisions: which crops to plant, what irrigation to build, where pests may spread, and whether a region remains suitable for a crop.

Climate change shifts the background conditions that farmers depend on.

### Compound Events
A compound event happens when multiple hazards or stresses combine. Heat plus drought is worse than heat alone. Flooding plus power outage can damage cold storage. Drought plus high fertilizer prices can reduce yield and income.

Food systems are vulnerable to compound events because production, logistics, and access are connected. A single shock is hard. Several shocks at once can overwhelm backup plans.

Resilience planning should ask: what if two things go wrong together?

### Heat And Photosynthesis
Photosynthesis depends on enzymes and leaf gas exchange. Heat can disturb both. If temperatures are too high, photosynthesis may slow while respiration continues. The plant may use more energy just to stay alive.

Heat can also reduce pollen viability in some crops, causing fewer fruits or grains. This means heat during a sensitive flowering period can be more damaging than heat at another time.

For students: timing matters. A climate hazard can be worse if it hits a vulnerable growth stage.

### Heat And Cold Chain
Hotter weather also affects logistics. Refrigerated trucks, cold rooms, and retail chillers must work harder. Power demand may rise. If cooling fails, food can spoil faster.

This connects climate risk to W10. A warming world may need stronger cold-chain planning, but cooling also uses energy, so efficiency matters.

### Water Quality During Drought And Flood
Drought can concentrate salts and pollutants in water. It can make irrigation water more expensive or unavailable. Floods can carry pathogens, sewage, chemicals, and debris into fields and storage areas.

So water stress includes quantity and quality. A farm may have water, but not water that is safe or suitable.

### Soil As A Resilience Tool
Healthy soil can improve water infiltration, water holding, root growth, and nutrient cycling. Practices such as cover crops, compost, reduced erosion, mulching, and organic matter management can support resilience.

Soil is not a complete shield against extreme climate, but it can reduce vulnerability. A soil that holds water better may help crops during dry periods.

### Crop Diversity And Genetic Diversity
Crop diversity means growing more than one crop or variety. Genetic diversity means variation within crops. Diversity can reduce risk because not every crop responds the same way to heat, pests, or disease.

If a farm grows only one variety, one disease or heat-sensitive stage can cause large loss. Diversity can make management more complex, but it gives options.

### Protected Cultivation As Adaptation
Shade nets, greenhouses, screenhouses, and indoor farms can protect crops from some hazards. Shade can reduce heat stress. Screens can reduce pests. Greenhouses can protect from heavy rain. Indoor farms can avoid some outdoor shocks.

But protected cultivation can fail during power outages, heat buildup, disease spread, or high energy costs. Adaptation tools need backup plans.

### Early Warning To Early Action
Early warning is only the first half. Early action is the second half. A heat warning may lead to shade, irrigation, changed work hours, moving animals, checking cold storage, or changing delivery schedules.

If a warning does not lead to action, it has little value. This repeats the data lesson from W07.

### Resilience At Different Scales
Plant scale: roots, stomata, heat tolerance.

Farm scale: irrigation, shade, crop choice, soil management.

Community scale: shared water, local storage, farmer support, emergency plans.

Country scale: import diversification, stockpiles, climate services, food safety systems.

Global scale: trade, climate mitigation, research cooperation, humanitarian response.

Mission Meals models tiny actions, but those actions can represent resilience at bigger scales.

### Climate Mission Design Checklist
For any climate-resilience mission, ask:

1. What is the hazard?
2. What is exposed?
3. What makes it vulnerable?
4. What adaptation does the robot model?
5. What tradeoff does the adaptation create?
6. What evidence shows the robot can perform the response reliably?

This checklist keeps climate missions from becoming vague.


## Additional Guided Reading: Climate Resilience Stories

### Story 1: Heat During Flowering
A crop may look healthy, but if extreme heat occurs during flowering, pollination may fail and yield can drop. This shows why timing matters. A short heat event can be serious if it hits a sensitive stage.

Robot model: move a shade resource to a flowering crop zone during a heat warning.

### Story 2: Drought And Feed Prices
Drought can reduce grain harvest. Grain is used for both human food and animal feed. If feed becomes expensive, eggs, dairy, meat, and aquaculture costs may rise.

Robot model: deliver emergency feed or move alternative supply markers.

### Story 3: Flood And Food Safety
Floodwater can contaminate fields, storage areas, or roads. Food that touched contaminated water may need to be discarded even if it looks normal.

Robot model: separate flood-zone food markers from safe food markers.

### Story 4: Heat And Workers
Outdoor farm workers may need shade, water, rest, and safer schedules during extreme heat. A food system depends on worker safety.

Robot model: move work tasks to cooler time markers or deliver water/shade resources.

### Story 5: Power Outage In A Cold Chain
A storm can interrupt electricity. Cold rooms and freezers may warm. Backup power, temperature monitoring, and quick decisions become important.

Robot model: move cold-chain items to backup storage.

### Adaptation Has Limits
Adaptation helps reduce harm, but it cannot solve every level of climate change. Some places may become unsuitable for certain crops. Some heat events may exceed what workers or animals can safely tolerate. Some water sources may decline.

This is why mitigation matters too. Food systems must adapt to current and near-future risks while also reducing emissions that increase future risks.

### Maladaptation
Maladaptation means an action that tries to help but creates new problems or increases future risk. For example, using huge amounts of energy for cooling without considering emissions may reduce heat risk locally but add climate pressure. Irrigation without water planning can deplete groundwater. A wall that protects one area from flooding may worsen flooding elsewhere.

Good adaptation thinks about long-term system effects.

### Climate Resilience And Mission Meals
Climate resilience can appear in many Mission Meals tasks: backup food routes, protected farms, water delivery, heat warning, cold-chain protection, flood safety sorting, or emergency stockpiles.

A strong explanation should include the risk chain:

```text
hazard -> exposed food-system part -> possible harm -> adaptation -> robot model -> test evidence
```

This chain prevents vague statements like "our robot helps climate." It shows exactly what part of climate resilience you are modelling.


## Final Climate Resilience Reflection

Climate resilience is not one machine or one policy. It is a pattern of preparation, monitoring, diversity, backup, adaptation, and recovery. It asks what can go wrong and how the system can keep feeding people safely.

This week also teaches humility. Some shocks are too large for a small tool to solve. A robot can model one response, but climate resilience also needs farmers, scientists, governments, communities, businesses, and families.

## Tiny Resilience Practice

Choose a food you eat often. Imagine a heat wave, flood, drought, or transport disruption affects it. Write a risk chain:

```text
hazard -> food-system part -> harm -> response
```

Example:

```text
heat wave -> leafy vegetable farm -> wilting and lower yield -> shade, irrigation, and backup supply
```

Then turn the response into a robot mission. The robot might move a shade marker, deliver water, choose a backup route, protect cold storage, or sort unsafe flood-zone food.

This practice is important because climate explanations can become too big. A risk chain makes the idea small enough to model while still connected to the real world.


A final practical rule: resilience is tested when conditions are not perfect. In robotics, test the backup route, the low-battery run, the shifted object, and the slower careful delivery. In food systems, planners test emergency stock, alternative suppliers, heat response, cold-chain backup, and communication plans.

If your climate mission has only a normal route, it is not really a resilience mission yet. Add a disruption marker and show how the robot responds. Then record whether the response works repeatedly.

Resilience also includes recovery time. Two systems may both fail during a shock, but one recovers in one hour and another takes one month. Faster recovery can protect food access and reduce waste. In robotics, recovery time is the time needed to reset, repair, or rerun a mission. A resilient team notices failures quickly, understands them, and returns to reliable action. Climate resilience also asks who is most affected. A heat wave may hurt outdoor workers, small farms without irrigation, families facing high prices, and cold-chain businesses with high energy costs. A fair resilience plan pays attention to vulnerable people, not only total food output. Robots can model tasks, but people remain the reason resilience matters. When you write a climate mission, avoid hopeless language. The goal is not to scare students. The goal is to show that systems can prepare, reduce harm, protect people, and learn. Serious risk and practical action should appear together in the same explanation. For your final projects, connect climate resilience to a concrete action: shade, water, backup route, safe sorting, cooling, stockpile, or early warning. Concrete actions are easier to build, test, and explain than giant claims about saving the planet. A small, tested response is more useful than a huge promise. Show what your robot can actually do, then explain what larger system idea it represents. Test the response under more than one condition so the resilience claim has evidence.

## Reading Recap
Climate risk changes food systems because crops, animals, workers, water, roads, storage, and electricity can all be affected. Heat, drought, floods, storms, pests, and disease do not stay in one part of the system. A heatwave can stress farm workers, reduce yields, damage perishable food, raise cooling needs, and disrupt delivery times.

Resilience means preparing for these shocks before they happen. A robot model can show backup routes, shade resources, water-saving choices, emergency delivery, or risk sorting. The key is to explain the disruption clearly. A backup route is not just a longer path; it represents a system that keeps working when the normal path fails.

## Core Terms

### Climate Risk
The chance that climate conditions will harm people, ecosystems, or systems.

### Hazard
A potentially damaging event, such as heat, drought, flood, storm, or wildfire.

### Exposure
Being in the place or situation where a hazard can cause harm.

### Vulnerability
How easily a person, crop, animal, or system can be harmed.

### Extreme Heat
Very high temperatures that can harm health, crops, animals, or infrastructure.

### Heat Stress
Harmful stress on plants, animals, or people caused by high temperature.

### Water Stress
When water supply, quality, timing, or access is not enough for needs.

### Drought
A period of unusually low water availability.

### Flood
Too much water in a place, which can damage crops, soil, roads, and food safety.

### Adaptation
Changing systems or behaviour to reduce harm from climate impacts.

### Mitigation
Reducing greenhouse gas emissions or increasing carbon storage to slow climate change.

### Early-Warning System
A system that uses data and forecasts to warn people before danger becomes worse.

### Climate Service
Climate information prepared so people can make decisions.

### Resilience
The ability to prepare for, survive, and recover from shocks.
