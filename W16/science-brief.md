# W16 Science Brief - From Food System To Robot System

This brief keeps the main ideas from `science.md` for shorter review.

## Big Idea
A strong Mission Meals project does not only say, "Our robot moves objects." It says:

```text
Here is the food-system problem.
Here is the part we modelled.
Here is the robot task.
Here is the mechanism and program.
Here is our test evidence.
Here is what we would improve next.
```

W16 connects the whole course into one capstone story.

## Past, Present, And Future
In the past, people improved food systems with seed saving, irrigation, storage, preservation, animal power, trade, refrigeration, tools, and machines.

Today, food systems are global, technical, and sometimes fragile. Singapore depends on imports while building resilience through diversified sources, selected local production, stockpiles, safety checks, and partnerships. The world still faces hunger, food waste, climate risk, and unequal access to healthy diets.

In the future, food systems may use more automation, climate-resilient crops, controlled environment farms, precision data, better cold chains, alternative proteins, circular recovery, and food-rescue networks. A good future must also be safe, fair, nutritious, affordable, and responsible.

## Systems Thinking
**Systems thinking** means understanding how parts interact. If one part changes, other parts may change too.

Example:

```text
more indoor farming -> more local vegetables
more indoor farming -> more electricity demand
more electricity demand -> need energy planning
```

Good thinking asks what improves, what becomes harder, who benefits, who may be left out, and what evidence supports the claim.

## Scenario Thinking
A **scenario** is a possible future used for planning. It is not a prediction. You are not saying, "This will happen." You are saying, "If this happens, what should we design?"

Useful scenarios include heat and water stress, supply-chain disruption, more local farms, more automated logistics, food waste recovery, alternative proteins, and higher need for traceability.

A robot does not need to solve every scenario. Choose one clear scenario and model one useful action.

## Capstone Translation
A **capstone** project combines what you learned. The key translation is:

```text
science knowledge -> mission design -> robot system -> evidence -> explanation
```

Science without mission design stays abstract. A mission without a robot stays imaginary. A robot without evidence stays uncertain. Evidence without explanation stays silent.

## Four Strong Story Types
A production story shows the robot supporting plant or food production, such as moving trays, delivering water, or sorting plant health.

A labour story shows the robot reducing repeated or tiring work, such as carrying, sorting, pushing, or handling objects.

A logistics story shows the robot moving food safely through storage, cold chain, traceability, or delivery routes.

A waste and resilience story shows the robot rescuing edible food, sorting waste streams, handling compost, responding to climate disruption, or using a backup route.

A team can combine ideas, but the explanation must still be clear.

## Evidence-Based Claim
Use this pattern:

```text
Our project models ___, a problem in ___.
The robot represents this by ___.
The key science idea is ___.
The mechanism works by ___.
The program uses ___.
Our evidence is ___ successful runs out of ___.
The main failure mode was ___.
Our next improvement is ___.
```

This pattern keeps the project honest and specific.

## Ethics And Stakeholders
**Ethics** means asking what is safe, fair, responsible, and good for people and the environment.

A **stakeholder** is a person or group affected by a system. Stakeholders may include farmers, workers, consumers, families, students, food safety officers, shops, drivers, scientists, government agencies, and the environment.

Ask: Who benefits? Who pays? Is the food safe? What happens to workers? Is data trustworthy? What if the system fails? Does the solution help access or only look impressive?

Ethics is part of design, not an extra decoration.

## Tradeoffs And Boundaries
A **tradeoff** means improving one thing may make another thing harder. Faster delivery may increase drops. More packaging may protect food but create waste. A backup route may be slower but safer.

A model boundary says what the project includes and does not include. Example: "Our model includes tray movement and inspection markers. It does not model the full biology or economics of a farm." Boundaries make claims stronger because they prevent exaggeration.

## Mission Meals Connections
A capstone should connect the robot to a real food-system problem, a clear model, a mechanism, program logic, test evidence, one tradeoff, and one future improvement.

A weak claim says, "Our robot saves food." A stronger claim says, "Our robot models a sorting system that sends safe soon-to-expire food to rescue, unsafe food to inspection, and scraps to compost. This shows that waste reduction needs sorting, timing, safety, and logistics."

## Extra Details To Remember
A strong problem statement is specific. "Food is important" is too broad. "Cities need reliable ways to move perishable food before it spoils during heat and transport delays" is stronger. It gives the robot a job.

A model boundary keeps the project honest. You can say what your model includes and what it does not include. For example, a tray mission may model movement and inspection, but not the full biology or business cost of a real farm.

A storyboard can help organize the final explanation: food-system problem, real-world science, mission model, mechanism, program logic, test evidence, failure mode, and improvement.

A one-minute pitch should be short and clear: our project focuses on this problem, it matters because this, our robot models it by this, our key science idea is this, our evidence is this, and our next improvement is this.

Sources should support ideas, not decorate the page. Use a source because it helped you understand a fact or rule. Read it, think, and explain in your own words.

## Review Reminder
A capstone is strongest when the robot action, science idea, and evidence all match.

## Key Takeaways
The final project is a clear model, not a giant promise. A small robot cannot solve world hunger, climate change, or food waste by itself. It can model one useful idea inside a larger system and show evidence that the model works.

Final check: write one sentence for the food-system problem, one sentence for the robot model, one evidence-based claim, one tradeoff, and one future improvement.
