## The One-Minute Idea
A strong Mission Meals project does not say, "Our robot moves objects." It says:

```text
Here is the food-system problem.
Here is the part we modelled.
Here is the robot task.
Here is the mechanism and program.
Here is our test evidence.
Here is what we would improve next.
```

For an eight-year-old: your robot should have a reason, a job, proof, and a next step.

## Past, Present, Future

### Past
Food systems grew from local survival systems into large agricultural and trade systems. Tools helped humans control water, labour, storage, transport, and information.

People solved food problems with the tools they had: seed saving, irrigation, preservation, animal power, storage, trade, machines, refrigeration, and scientific farming.

### Present
Food systems are global, technical, and fragile in some places. Singapore depends on imports but builds resilience through diversification, local production, stockpiling, and partnerships. The world still faces hunger, waste, climate risk, and unequal access to healthy diets.

Robots and data tools already help in farms, warehouses, research labs, and factories, but they are only parts of a larger system.

### Future
Future food systems may be more local in some ways, more global in others, more automated, more data-driven, more climate-aware, and more diverse in protein sources. Students may help design parts of that future through robotics, science, food safety, logistics, farming, policy, or education.

A good future is not only high-tech. It is safe, fair, nutritious, affordable, resilient, and respectful of people and the environment.

## Deep Explanation

### 1. Systems Thinking Connects Parts
A system is a set of parts that interact. If you change one part, others may change too.

Example:

```text
More indoor farming -> more local vegetables
More indoor farming -> more electricity demand
More electricity demand -> need energy planning
```

Good thinking follows the connections. It asks what improves, what becomes harder, who benefits, and what evidence supports the claim.

### 2. A Scenario Is Not A Prediction
A scenario is a possible future used for planning. You do not say, "This will happen." You say, "If this happens, what should we design?"

Useful food-system scenarios:

- high heat and water stress,
- more local controlled-environment farms,
- more automated food logistics,
- more alternative proteins,
- more food waste recovery,
- supply-chain disruption,
- more demand for traceability,
- more need for safe food rescue.

Scenarios help teams test ideas without pretending to know the future exactly.

### 3. A Robot Story Needs Evidence
A robot story should include:

- problem,
- model,
- mechanism,
- code logic,
- test results,
- failure modes,
- improvement plan.

Without evidence, the story is only an idea. With evidence, it becomes an engineering claim.

### 4. Technology Has Ethics
Ethics means asking what is fair, safe, responsible, and good for people and the environment. Ask:

- Who benefits?
- Who pays?
- What happens to workers?
- Is the food safe?
- Is the data trustworthy?
- What if the system fails?
- Does the solution create waste or save waste?
- Does it help access or only look impressive?

Ethics is not an extra topic after engineering. It is part of responsible design.

### 5. Capstone Thinking Is Translation
Your final task is translation:

```text
science knowledge -> mission design -> robot system -> evidence -> explanation
```

Each arrow matters. Science without mission design stays abstract. Mission design without robot system stays imaginary. Robot system without evidence stays uncertain. Evidence without explanation stays silent.

### 7. Four Strong Capstone Story Types
Production story: The robot supports plant or food production by moving trays, delivering water, sorting plant health, or activating a farm station.

Labour story: The robot reduces repetitive work by carrying, sorting, pushing, or handling objects reliably.

Logistics story: The robot moves food safely through storage, cold chain, traceability, or delivery routes.

Waste and resilience story: The robot sorts surplus, rescues edible food, handles compost streams, or responds to climate disruption.

A team can combine story types, but the explanation should still be clear.

### 8. Evidence-Based Claim Template
Use this template:

```text
Our project models ___, a problem in ___.
The robot represents this by ___.
The key science idea is ___.
The mechanism works by ___.
The program uses ___.
Our evidence is ___ successful runs out of ___.
The main failure mode was ___.
Our next improvement is ___.
```

This template prevents vague claims and connects every part of the project.

### 9. Tradeoffs Make The Explanation Stronger
Do not hide tradeoffs. Mention them clearly.

Example: "A wider gripper protects the tray from tipping, but it makes the robot larger and harder to turn."

Example: "Indoor farming can save land for selected crops, but lighting and cooling use energy."

Example: "A faster delivery route saves time, but our tests showed it dropped the box more often."

Tradeoffs show that you understand engineering as real decision-making.

### 10. Future Improvement Should Be Specific
Weak improvement: make it better.

Stronger improvement: widen the guide by two studs so the crate enters more reliably.

Strongest improvement: widen the guide by two studs, then run the shifted-object test again to see whether success improves from 7/10 to at least 9/10.

A future improvement should be testable.

### 11. Capstone Scenario Examples
Scenario 1: Singapore needs more reliable local leafy greens during supply disruption. Robot model: tray movement and plant inspection. Evidence: 10-run tray delivery and sorting test.

Scenario 2: A cold-chain delay risks seafood spoilage. Robot model: cold box delivery to correct station with time limit. Evidence: delivery accuracy and release tests.

Scenario 3: A school wants to reduce safe edible food waste. Robot model: sort safe surplus to rescue zone and inedible scraps to compost zone. Evidence: sorting accuracy.

Scenario 4: A heat wave threatens crop production. Robot model: move shade or water resources to stressed crop zones. Evidence: response route reliability.

Scenario 5: A future protein system needs safety assessment. Robot model: move novel food through production, inspection, and labelling stations. Evidence: correct sequence and sorting.


## Expanded Knowledge Notes

### The Capstone Is Not A Poster After The Robot
The capstone explanation should guide the robot design from the beginning. If you wait until the end to invent a story, the robot may not match the science.

Start with the problem. Then choose the model. Then design the robot. Then test evidence. Then write the explanation. The story and the robot should grow together.

### The Problem Statement
A problem statement should be specific.

Weak: Food security is important.

Stronger: Fresh vegetables can be disrupted by limited land, climate stress, and logistics delays.

Strongest: Our model focuses on moving local hydroponic vegetable trays safely from a production zone to a market zone, representing how controlled-environment farms need reliable tray handling and logistics.

A specific problem statement helps everyone understand the robot's job.

### The Model Boundary
A model boundary says what your model includes and does not include. This is honest science.

Example: Our model includes tray movement and inspection markers. It does not model the full biology of plant growth or the economics of farm operation.

Boundaries make your explanation stronger because they prevent exaggeration.

### Stakeholders
Stakeholders are people or groups affected by a system. In Mission Meals, stakeholders may include students, farmers, workers, consumers, food safety officers, retailers, families, and people facing food insecurity.

Naming stakeholders helps you think ethically. A robot that helps farms but makes food too expensive may not help consumers. A robot that moves fast but is unsafe near workers is not responsible.

### Evidence Chain
An evidence chain connects tests to claims:

```text
claim -> measurement -> test condition -> result -> conclusion
```

Example:

```text
claim: our robot can deliver fragile produce reliably
measurement: successful tray delivery without tipping
condition: 10 runs from marked start
result: 9 successful, 1 tip during shifted object test
conclusion: reliable under normal setup, needs wider guide for shifted objects
```

This is much stronger than saying "it works."

### Storyboard Your Explanation
A storyboard is a sequence of frames. You can storyboard your capstone explanation:

1. Food-system problem.
2. Real-world science.
3. Mission model.
4. Robot mechanism.
5. Program logic.
6. Test evidence.
7. Failure mode.
8. Improvement.

This helps you keep the explanation in order.

### Scenario Planning
Scenario planning is useful when the future is uncertain. Instead of predicting one future, you explore several possible futures.

Example scenarios:

- climate stress increases local production need,
- logistics disruption delays imported food,
- food waste reduction becomes a school priority,
- alternative protein needs safety sorting,
- farms need more automation because labour is limited.

Your robot does not need to solve all scenarios. Choose one and model a clear action.

### Ethics In The Final Explanation
Ethics should be concrete. Instead of saying "ethics is important," name the issue.

Worker safety: Does the robot move safely near people?

Food safety: Does the system keep unsafe food away from people?

Access: Could the solution help food reach people, or only make technology look impressive?

Environment: Does the solution reduce waste, water use, energy use, or emissions?

Honesty: Does the team avoid claiming more than the model shows?

### The One-Minute Pitch
A one-minute pitch should be clear:

```text
Our project focuses on ___.
This matters because ___.
Our robot models this by ___.
The key science idea is ___.
We tested it by ___.
Our result was ___.
Our next improvement is ___.
```

Practise until every team member can explain it.

### The Three-Minute Deep Explanation
A longer explanation can add details:

- past: how people handled this problem before,
- present: current data or technology,
- future: challenge or research direction,
- terms: important vocabulary,
- mechanism: end-effector and movement,
- program: sequence, condition, variable, state,
- evidence: test table,
- ethics: tradeoff and stakeholder.

This connects back to the user's original learning goal: past, present, future, terms, science, and robotics together.

### Capstone Quality Checklist
A strong capstone has:

- one clear food-system problem,
- one clear mission model,
- accurate vocabulary,
- child-friendly explanation,
- one or more reliable sources,
- a mechanism matched to object properties,
- clear program logic,
- 10-run evidence,
- named failure modes,
- a realistic improvement plan,
- one tradeoff,
- one ethical question.

### What Not To Say
Avoid exaggerated claims:

- "Our robot solves world hunger."
- "Vertical farms solve all food problems."
- "AI will fix farming."
- "Food waste can all become compost, so it is fine."
- "Future foods are automatically better."

Replace them with evidence-based claims:

- "Our robot models one logistics action that can support food access."
- "Indoor farms can support selected local crops, but energy is a tradeoff."
- "Sensors can support decisions if data is reliable."
- "Prevention and safe rescue should come before composting edible food."
- "Future foods need safety assessment and consumer trust."

### Final Reflection Questions
Ask yourself:

1. What did I learn about food systems that changed how I see a meal?
2. What did I learn about robots that changed how I build mechanisms?
3. What term can I now explain clearly?
4. What evidence did our team collect?
5. What tradeoff did our team face?
6. What would I improve with one more month?

These questions turn the course into personal understanding.

## Further Deep-Dive Notes

### A Capstone Is A Clear Model, Not A Poster
The final project should not only list facts about food. It should model a food-system challenge and show how a robot could help. A model is a simplified version of reality. A good model keeps the important relationships and removes details that are not needed for the current explanation.

For example, a real food-rescue system includes farms, supermarkets, restaurants, volunteers, storage, vehicles, apps, food-safety rules, and people who need meals. A student robot model cannot include everything. It might include only three states of food: edible, urgent, and unsafe. It might include one rescue vehicle, one cold-storage zone, and one compost zone. That is acceptable if the team can explain what each part represents.

A weak capstone says, "Our robot saves food." A stronger capstone says, "Our robot models a decision system that sends edible soon-to-expire food to rescue, unsafe food to inspection, and scraps to compost. This shows that preventing waste requires sorting, timing, safety, and logistics."

### Building A Problem Statement
A problem statement explains the challenge in one or two sentences. It should be specific enough that a robot mission can respond to it.

Weak statement:

```text
Food is important.
```

Better statement:

```text
Cities need reliable ways to move perishable food before it spoils, especially during heat and transport delays.
```

Strong student statement:

```text
Our project models how a smart food-rescue system can sort urgent edible food, protect it in cold storage, and deliver it quickly before it becomes waste.
```

A strong statement includes a food-system problem, a reason it matters, and a direction for action.

### The Evidence Chain
An evidence chain connects research, design, robot behavior, and explanation. Each link should make sense.

```text
research fact -> design decision -> robot action -> measured result -> explanation
```

Example:

```text
research fact: perishable food can become unsafe when storage conditions fail
design decision: include a cold-chain checkpoint
robot action: move temperature-sensitive food through the cold zone
measured result: robot delivered the protected token 9/10 times
explanation: reliable cold-chain handling can reduce loss and protect safety
```

Another example:

```text
research fact: food systems face climate disruptions such as heat and blocked routes
design decision: include a backup route
robot action: detect obstacle marker and choose alternate path
measured result: backup route took longer but still delivered food
explanation: resilient systems may trade speed for reliability
```

This chain prevents the capstone from becoming disconnected. The science should explain the robot. The robot should make the science visible.

### Stakeholders: Who Is Affected?
A stakeholder is a person or group affected by a system. Food-system stakeholders may include farmers, fishers, warehouse workers, drivers, shop owners, families, children, elderly people, government agencies, scientists, engineers, and the environment.

When designing a capstone, ask what each stakeholder needs. A farmer may need fair prices and climate support. A consumer may need safe, affordable food. A food-rescue group may need quick information and reliable pickup. A regulator may need safety evidence. The environment needs less pollution and less waste.

A robot model can include stakeholders through roles. A pickup zone can represent farms. A rescue zone can represent community kitchens. An inspection zone can represent regulators. A waste zone can represent environmental impact. A mission card can represent a stakeholder request.

### Tradeoffs Make The Project More Honest
A tradeoff means improving one thing may make another thing harder. Honest projects include tradeoffs.

Examples:

1. Faster delivery may reduce spoilage, but it may increase robot errors.
2. More packaging may protect food, but it may create waste.
3. Local production may improve resilience, but it may cost more at first.
4. Automation may improve consistency, but people still need to understand and trust it.
5. A backup route may avoid a blocked path, but it may take longer.
6. A strict safety rule may reject some edible food, but it protects people from risk.

A team that discusses tradeoffs sounds more scientific than a team that says every idea is perfect. Real engineering is often about choosing the best balance for a clear goal.

### Past, Present, Future In One Story
The 16-week plan asks you to connect history, current science, and future possibilities. Your final explanation can use this simple structure:

Past:

```text
Humans improved food supply through farming, irrigation, storage, transport, refrigeration, and scientific breeding.
```

Present:

```text
Today the world has advanced sensors, robotics, data systems, controlled-environment agriculture, alternative proteins, and food-rescue networks, but hunger, waste, climate stress, and supply-chain weakness still exist.
```

Future:

```text
Future food systems may use more automation, climate-resilient crops, precision fermentation, cultivated foods, better cold chains, circular nutrient systems, and smarter rescue networks.
```

Then connect your robot:

```text
Our robot is a small model of this future because it senses, decides, transports, sorts, and responds to disruption.
```

This helps judges and classmates understand why a table-top mission is connected to a world problem.

### Example Capstone Scenario 1: Heatwave Food Rescue
Scenario: A city is experiencing a heatwave. Perishable food at a market must be rescued quickly. Some food is still safe, some needs cold storage, and some must be inspected.

Science ideas:

1. Heat can increase spoilage risk.
2. Cold chains protect perishable food.
3. Food rescue must balance speed and safety.
4. Sensors and rules help people make decisions.

Robot model:

1. The robot reads a temperature marker.
2. It moves urgent food to cold storage.
3. It sends risky food to inspection.
4. It uses a backup route if the normal route is blocked.
5. It records success rate and delivery time.

Capstone claim:

```text
Our robot models how a resilient food-rescue system can use sensing, sorting, cold storage, and backup routing to reduce waste during heat stress.
```

### Example Capstone Scenario 2: Future Protein Factory
Scenario: A city wants more local protein sources. The system includes plant protein, fermentation, and safety checks.

Science ideas:

1. Protein is made from amino acids.
2. Alternative proteins can reduce pressure on some traditional systems.
3. Novel foods need safety evidence.
4. Consumers need clear labels and trust.

Robot model:

1. The robot collects ingredient tokens.
2. It checks a safety marker.
3. It sends approved products to the market.
4. It sends missing-label products to review.
5. It explains one tradeoff for each protein option.

Capstone claim:

```text
Our robot models a future-food production line where new protein sources are useful only when nutrition, safety, labelling, and trust are handled together.
```

### Source Use In The Final Project
Use sources to support your ideas, not to decorate the page. A good source note says what the source helped you understand.

Examples:

1. [FAO food systems work](https://www.fao.org/food-systems/en/) helped us understand that food includes production, transport, processing, consumption, and waste.
2. [UNEP Food Waste Index](https://www.unep.org/resources/publication/food-waste-index-report-2024) helped us understand that waste happens in homes, food service, and retail.
3. [Singapore Food Agency resources](https://www.sfa.gov.sg/food-for-thought/article/detail/alternative-proteins) helped us understand why alternative proteins need safety assessment and public trust.
4. [WMO and FAO heat guidance](https://wmo.int/publication-series/heat-health-risks-agricultural-workers) helped us understand that heat affects people who work in food systems.

Do not copy long sentences. Read, think, and explain in your own words. If a fact is important, link the source.

### Final Presentation Logic
A clear final explanation can follow this order:

1. Name the food-system problem.
2. Explain why it matters.
3. Define the key scientific terms.
4. Show how the robot models the system.
5. Present one piece of evidence from testing.
6. Explain one tradeoff.
7. Describe one future improvement.

This structure is short enough for students to remember and strong enough to show serious learning.

### Final Quality Checklist
Before presenting, check:

1. Can a visitor understand the problem in 20 seconds?
2. Does every robot mission action represent a science idea?
3. Are important terms explained clearly?
4. Are links or sources included for major claims?
5. Is there evidence from robot testing?
6. Does the team mention at least one tradeoff?
7. Does the project connect past, present, and future?
8. Can each student explain one part without reading a script?

If the answer is yes, the capstone is not only a robot run. It is a learning story.

## Core Terms

### Systems Thinking
Understanding how parts interact inside a larger whole.

### Scenario
A possible future used for planning and design.

### Capstone
A final project that combines many things learned earlier.

### Prototype
An early version used for learning and testing.

### Evidence-Based Claim
A statement supported by records, tests, or observations.

### Tradeoff
A choice where improving one thing may make another thing harder.

### Ethics
Thinking about safety, fairness, responsibility, and consequences.

### Stakeholder
A person or group affected by a system or decision.

### Resilience
The ability to prepare for, survive, and recover from shocks.

### Improvement Plan
A clear next step based on what testing showed.

### Future Scenario
A structured story about a possible future used to test ideas.

### Translation
Changing knowledge from one form into another, such as food-system science into robot mission design.
